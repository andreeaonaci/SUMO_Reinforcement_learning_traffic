\begin{abstract}
A single reinforcement learning policy that controls intersections of different
geometry must produce an action vector of fixed width. The common construction binds
output row $k$ to phase index $k$ and masks rows that do not exist at a given
intersection. This paper shows that this positional indexing is the main obstacle to
zero-shot transfer across intersection topologies: phase orderings are defined
independently for each network, so one output row is trained on unrelated movements.
A control experiment in which every selectable row is fully trained shows that the
indexed policy still fails ($-9028.88$ against $-9296.84$ with untrained rows present).
A phase-relational readout is proposed that scores each candidate phase from a
ten-dimensional descriptor derived automatically from the simulator's connection table,
so that no per-intersection configuration is required and the network's own signal
program is kept. Trained with federated learning on three RESCO networks and evaluated
on an unseen network, the readout outperforms the indexed one by four orders of
magnitude on five configurations (six seeds each). At the benchmark's signal timing it
achieves lower waiting time than max pressure on every seed at equal throughput, with
equal average delay, and it outperforms the released implementation of a concurrent
method on all three evaluated networks. Re-running the two strongest of 41 earlier
interventions with the new readout reverses both, which indicates that results obtained
with the indexed readout mainly reflected its limitation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Reinforcement learning (RL) controllers for traffic signals outperform fixed-time and
actuated control on the networks they are trained
on~\cite{intellilight,presslight,colight}. For deployment, a more useful property is
\emph{zero-shot transfer across topology}: a controller trained on a small set of
instrumented intersections should operate at intersections it has never observed,
without retraining. This differs from transfer across demand on a fixed network and
remains an open problem.

Transfer across topology imposes a structural requirement. Intersections differ in
their number of signal phases, from two to eight in the networks used here, while a
shared policy must emit a vector of fixed width. The standard construction pads every
action space to a common maximum $A_{\max}$ and applies a binary mask of legal actions.
Output row $k$ is thereby bound to ``phase index $k$'' at whichever intersection is
controlled. Phase orderings, however, are defined per network by whoever built it and
carry no meaning across networks. Row $k$ is therefore trained on whatever movements
occupy slot $k$ in each training network.

Phase-invariant readouts have been proposed before~\cite{frap,mplight,attendlight}, and
a concurrent method scores movements with a graph network~\cite{graphiface}. These works
replace the indexed readout without measuring why it fails, and they either require a
hand-written phase-to-movement table for every intersection~\cite{frap,mplight} or
replace the network's signal program with synthesized phases~\cite{graphiface}.

This paper makes the following contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A control experiment (Section~\ref{sec:control}) that separates the two possible
      causes of failure of the indexed readout, untrained rows and conflicting row
      semantics, and shows that the failure persists when every selectable row is fully
      trained.
\item A phase-relational readout (Section~\ref{sec:method}) whose phase descriptor is
      derived from the simulator's connection table. It needs no per-intersection
      configuration and keeps the network's signal program.
\item An evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:results}) on five zero-shot configurations, at the
      RESCO benchmark's settings, against max pressure, fixed time, a ported FRAP, the
      published RESCO baselines and the released code of the concurrent method.
\item A re-evaluation of 41 interventions previously tested with the indexed readout
      (Section~\ref{sec:inventory}), in which the two strongest reverse, and a record of
      six evaluation artifacts (Section~\ref{sec:artifacts}).
\end{enumerate}
Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work, Section~\ref{sec:problem} formalises the
problem, Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the method, Section~\ref{sec:setup} the
experimental setup, Section~\ref{sec:results} the results and
Section~\ref{sec:analysis} further analysis. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses
limitations and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Reinforcement learning for traffic signal control}
Deep $Q$-learning was applied to isolated intersections by
IntelliLight~\cite{intellilight}, and later work addressed coordination between
intersections~\cite{presslight,colight}. A survey~\cite{survey} notes that gains are
usually reported on the network a method was trained on. The RESCO
benchmark~\cite{resco} reports that several published methods underperform well-tuned
heuristics under realistic demand. The learner used in this paper follows standard
practice~\cite{dqn,ddqn,dueling}; the object of study is the readout.

\subsection{Phase representation}
FRAP~\cite{frap} represents a phase as a pair of competing movements and scores all
ordered pairs through a competition mask, which gives invariance to flip and rotation.
MPLight~\cite{mplight} uses FRAP as backbone with pressure as state and reward, and
scales to thousands of signals. AttendLight~\cite{attendlight} applies attention over
lanes and phases and generalises when similar configurations are present in training.
FRAP and MPLight require, for every scenario and signal, a table mapping local phase
indices to a global movement vocabulary; RESCO's documentation states that movements
``must be remapped to correspond to the same action in each agent'', and its
\texttt{cologne3} configuration contains three such hand-written dictionaries. The
descriptor of Section~\ref{sec:descriptors} is derived at run time instead. This is a
difference in capability, not a performance claim.

\subsection{Transfer and generalisation}
MetaLight~\cite{metalight} adapts to new intersections by meta-learning and therefore
assumes gradient steps on the target. X-Light~\cite{xlight} learns across cities with a
hierarchical transformer. Inductive graph formulations~\cite{igrl} and
TransferLight~\cite{transferlight} target deployment on unseen networks, the latter
through domain randomisation over generated road networks. Concurrently, Braun~\cite{graphiface}
scores individual movements with a shared graph network and maps the scores to each
junction's phase set through an incidence matrix, so parameter shapes are independent
of the number of phases. That method is trained with a policy-gradient algorithm and
constructs its own phase sets; it is compared directly in Section~\ref{sec:braun}.
Neither phase-invariant scoring, nor zero-shot transfer, nor federated training is
claimed as new here; the contribution is the control of Section~\ref{sec:control} and
the configuration-free readout.

\subsection{Federated and curriculum training}
Aggregation follows FedAvg~\cite{fedavg}; FedProx~\cite{fedprox} and a Reptile-style
meta-aggregation~\cite{reptile} are among the re-evaluated interventions. Federated
formulations of signal control~\cite{hfrl} address privacy and scale, including
variants that cluster intersections before averaging. Ordered client curricula were
studied by Vahidian \emph{et al.}~\cite{curricula}; the curriculum arms of
Table~\ref{tab:curriculum} follow that construction, and only their behaviour under the
corrected readout is reported here.

\section{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:problem}

Let $\mathcal{C}$ be a set of road networks. Network $c\in\mathcal{C}$ contains
signalised intersections $i\in\mathcal{I}_c$. Each intersection is observed every
$\Delta t=5$\,s with local observation $o_i$, action set $\mathcal{A}_i$ of size $A_i$
equal to its number of green phases, and reward $r_i$ given by the negative change in
cumulative waiting time over its incoming lanes.

A shared policy is one parameter vector $\theta$ used at every intersection of every
network. Since $A_i$ varies, $\theta$ must induce a function of common output width
$A_{\max}=\max_i A_i$ with mask $m_i\in\{0,1\}^{A_{\max}}$, $m_i[k]=1$ if and only if
$k<A_i$:
\begin{equation}
a_i \;=\; \arg\max_{k\,:\,m_i[k]=1}\; Q_\theta(o_i)[k].
\label{eq:argmax}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Positional indexing}
Let $\sigma_i(k)$ denote the set of movements served by phase $k$ at intersection $i$.
The standard readout is
\begin{equation}
Q_\theta(o_i) \;=\; W\,h_\theta(o_i) + b,
\qquad W\in\mathbb{R}^{A_{\max}\times d},
\label{eq:indexed}
\end{equation}
so row $W_k$ alone values action $k$ everywhere and is trained on
$\bigcup_i \sigma_i(k)$ over all training intersections. Transfer to an unseen
intersection $j$ requires $W_k$ to value $\sigma_j(k)$, but nothing in
\eqref{eq:indexed} relates $\sigma_j(k)$ to any $\sigma_i(k)$ seen in training. Two
failure modes follow. \emph{Untrained rows}: if $A_j>\max_i A_i$, rows
$k\ge\max_i A_i$ are never updated; on the rosters used here, $37.5\%$ of the holdout's
legal actions are scored by such rows. \emph{Semantic collision}: within the trained
range, $\sigma_i(k)$ differs across $i$. Index~1 denotes a protected left turn in
\texttt{arterial4x4} and \texttt{grid4x4} and a through movement in \texttt{cologne3}
and \texttt{ingolstadt7} (Fig.~\ref{fig:collision}). The first mode admits a simple
remedy (a wider head, or a training city with more phases); the second does not.
Section~\ref{sec:control} determines which one dominates.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Shared observation}
Every intersection in every network emits a tuple of identical shape
(Fig.~\ref{fig:obs}). Topology enters only through the numerical content of these
tensors; no part of the model branches on city or intersection type, and the policy
receives no city identifier.

\subsection{Phase descriptors}\label{sec:descriptors}
For candidate phase $a$ at intersection $i$, let $L_i(a)$ be the incoming lanes that
$a$ gives right of way, obtained by intersecting the phase's state string with the
controlled-links table, and $L^{\mathrm{out}}_i(a)$ the downstream lanes of those
movements. The descriptor $\phi_i(a)\in\mathbb{R}^{10}$ contains, in order: phase
pressure $\mathrm{clip}\big((q_{\mathrm{in}}-q_{\mathrm{out}})/10,\,\pm5\big)$;
normalised queue; normalised accumulated waiting time; mean occupancy; lane count; an
indicator that $a$ is currently displayed; the time elapsed in that phase if so; and the
fractions of green lanes whose movement is straight, left and right. Every entry is
computable at any signalised intersection from the simulator alone, and the last three
entries describe what a phase does in terms that recur across networks.

\subsection{Phase-relational \texorpdfstring{$Q$}{Q}-function}
Let $h_\theta(o_i)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$, $d=128$, be the state embedding of the shared
trunk: a lane encoder, a neighbour encoder with hop embeddings, masked multi-head
attention with the neighbour mask as key-padding mask, and a fusion MLP. The proposed
readout replaces \eqref{eq:indexed} with
\begin{equation}
Q_\theta(o_i)[a] \;=\; f_\psi\!\big([\,h_\theta(o_i)\,\|\,\phi_i(a)\,]\big),
\qquad f_\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d+10}\!\rightarrow\!\mathbb{R},
\label{eq:phaserel}
\end{equation}
where $f_\psi$ is a three-layer MLP applied to each candidate phase with shared weights.
The state embedding is computed once per decision, so the cost is one trunk evaluation
plus $A_i$ scorer evaluations.

Equation~\eqref{eq:phaserel} has no per-action parameters. The size of $\psi$ is
independent of $A_{\max}$, so no row can remain untrained; no index carries a meaning
that could conflict between networks; and $\max_a Q$ is defined for any $A_i$, including
counts not seen in training. In addition, because $\phi_i(a)$ contains per-phase
pressure, the max-pressure rule~\cite{varaiya} lies inside the hypothesis space of
\eqref{eq:phaserel}, whereas the indexed readout observes only an intersection-level
pressure and cannot represent it. Fig.~\ref{fig:readouts} compares the readouts.

\subsection{Federated training}
Cities train in parallel worker processes, each keeping its simulator, replay buffer and
optimiser state across rounds; only parameters are exchanged. Each round broadcasts
$\theta$, trains each city for $E$ local episodes, and aggregates by sample-count
weighting. For the indexed readout, row $k$ is averaged only over cities that use it;
readouts without per-action rows reduce to plain averaging. The aggregate is evaluated
after every round on a network excluded from training.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Networks and scenarios}\label{sec:scenarios}
Experiments use SUMO~\cite{sumo} and the RESCO~\cite{resco} networks
(Table~\ref{tab:networks}). Training uses \texttt{arterial4x4}, \texttt{cologne3} and
\texttt{ingolstadt7}; \texttt{grid4x4} is the holdout. The holdout's action space is

nominal demand. The latter was calibrated by the rule-based controllers' own waiting time
so that it neither saturates nor gridlocks them: fixed time degrades by a factor of 62
relative to nominal demand and max pressure doubles its waiting time.

\subsection{Signal timing}\label{sec:timing}
A controller selects a phase every 5\,s. Selecting the current phase keeps it green.
A switch inserts a yellow interval and is accepted only after the current phase has held
for the yellow interval plus a 5\,s minimum green; earlier requests are ignored. RESCO
specifies a 3\,s yellow, so the shortest phase lasts 10\,s with 7\,s of green. Part of
this study used the simulator default of 2\,s, which gives one additional second of
green per phase change for every controller. Results at 2\,s are therefore compared only
with each other; Table~\ref{tab:config} records the setting behind every result.

\subsection{Baselines}
\emph{Indexed readout} (A): the trunk of Section~\ref{sec:method} with \eqref{eq:indexed}.
\emph{FRAP} (C): ported from the RESCO source, with its competition mask verified to be
identical to the reference construction, and given the hand-written phase tables of every
network including the holdout. \emph{Max pressure} and \emph{fixed time}: rule-based
controllers evaluated with the same evaluator and seeds. \emph{IPPO} and \emph{IDQN}:
published RESCO results~\cite{resco}. \emph{Braun}~\cite{graphiface}: released code,
described in Section~\ref{sec:braun}.

\subsection{Metrics and statistics}
\emph{Reward} is the training signal (negative change in accumulated waiting time on the
incoming lanes, averaged over the evaluation); it is always negative, values closer to
zero are better, and it is used only for comparisons within this study. \emph{Waiting
time} is the time a vehicle spends below 0.1\,m/s. \emph{Average delay} is the time lost
relative to free-flow travel (SUMO \texttt{timeLoss}), the principal RESCO metric.
\emph{Trip time} is departure to arrival. These three are means over arrived vehicles
and lower is better. Because a controller that strands traffic improves them,
\emph{completion}, the fraction of vehicles departing in the evaluation window that
arrive, is reported alongside every delay value. Significance is
$|\Delta|/\mathrm{SE}$, unpaired with population standard deviation, with a threshold of
2. Drop-one influence is checked, and three-seed results are labelled screens.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Untrained rows versus positional indexing}\label{sec:control}
The two failure modes of Section~\ref{sec:problem} are separated by replacing the
holdout with a network whose phase count does not exceed the training maximum, so that
every row the holdout can select has been trained by every training city. If untrained
rows were the cause, performance would recover. Table~\ref{tab:control} shows that it
does not: both conditions gridlock and the difference lies within seed noise. The
indexed readout fails when fully trained, which also excludes the remedies suggested by
the untrained-row explanation.

\subsection{Comparison with rule-based control}
At the benchmark's timing the phase-relational readout exceeds max pressure on the
study's reward on every seed of both 3\,s rosters, at best and at final round, and at
equal throughput (98.9\% against 99.2\% completion). Measured from trip records, its
waiting time is lower on all six seeds (18.8\,s against 22.8\,s), while average delay is
tied (41.4$\pm$0.7\,s against 40.2\,s; three of six seeds lower). The advantage is thus
specific to waiting time, the quantity the policy is trained on.

\subsection{Training budget and FRAP}
The lower block of Table~\ref{tab:zeroshot} uses two and a half to four times the
standard training budget. The indexed readout remains four orders of magnitude behind,
and its final-round gap is larger than its best-round gap, which is not consistent with
undertraining. FRAP, given the hand-written tables, also transfers ($-0.253$). This
reproduces the effect with an independent implementation and shows that it belongs to
readouts addressed by movement semantics rather than to one feature set. The
phase-relational readout exceeds FRAP at the best round on all six seeds (4.06) without
any table; the final-round difference is not significant (1.71). FRAP degrades by a
factor of 3.49 from its best round against 2.19 for the proposed readout, and its
final-round spread across seeds is thirteen times larger.

\subsection{In-distribution performance}
Table~\ref{tab:indist} reports the RESCO networks used in training, at the benchmark's
settings. The indexed readout is also worse in-distribution (54.0 and 84.7\,s delay
against 21.4 and 35.2\,s) and completes fewer trips (77.6\% and 80.3\% against 91.6\%
and 95.4\%). Since the indexed rows are pulled towards conflicting targets during
training, the collision affects the training networks as well. Per-seed delay on
\texttt{cologne3} ranges from 19.7 to 23.0\,s for the proposed readout and from 25.7 to
99.1\,s for the indexed one.

On \texttt{cologne3} the proposed readout, trained federated on three networks with about
one tenth of RESCO's per-network budget, reaches a delay comparable to the published IPPO
and IDQN results and to max pressure, at lower completion (91.6\% against 98.6\% for
max pressure). RESCO does not report completion for its methods, so the difference to
them cannot be assessed from published data. On \texttt{ingolstadt7} max pressure has
lower delay (26.6 against 35.2\,s), although the proposed readout completes the most
trips (95.4\%).

\section{Analysis}\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Re-evaluation of earlier interventions}\label{sec:inventory}
Before the readout was identified as the cause, 41 interventions were evaluated with the
indexed readout on the same rosters and criterion (Table~\ref{tab:inventory}). Thirty-two
were null or negative, four inconclusive and five significant. Of the five, three are
test-time procedures that do not change the learned policy, and one, fine-tuning on the
target topology, was matched by a randomly initialised control. No change to
architecture, learning algorithm, aggregation rule or loss produced a durable
improvement. The interventions span largely independent mechanisms: ten aggregation
variants including the removal of federation (indistinguishable from federated training,
1.78/1.43), eleven architectural variants, five learning algorithms~\cite{ppo,munchausen,qrdqn,maml}
and five loss modifications, of which potential-based shaping~\cite{potential} was the
only significant one (about 2.5). A common null across these mechanisms indicates a
constraint upstream of all of them.

Fine-tuning on synthetic demand over the holdout topology was the largest effect with
the indexed readout: from a zero-shot reward of $-8664.73$ to $-1092.10$
($|\Delta|/\mathrm{SE}$ 72.78, four seeds). A randomly initialised network given the same
procedure reached $-406.85$ against $-693.84$ for the pretrained one, so the gain
reflected training on the target rather than transfer. With the phase-relational
readout, fine-tuning degrades the policy: from $-0.123$ to $-0.250$ after one round
(4.44) and $-0.205$ after two (3.75), with one of twelve runs improving by 0.02 and mean
waiting time rising from 0.29 to 0.67\,s. Across the six two-round runs, the correlation
between zero-shot reward and the change produced by adaptation is $r=-0.91$: adaptation
helps least where the initial policy is best. Both reversals are consistent with the
interventions having compensated for the indexing deficit. An alternative reading, that
adaptation to synthetic demand harms a near-optimal policy, is not excluded.

\subsection{Training-topology diversity}\label{sec:diversity}
Four synthetic irregular grids, generated by removing interior signalised junctions from
a lattice, were added to the three training networks, and evaluation used the
triple-demand holdout. Over six seeds, no measure favours the larger roster: best round
$-0.283$ against $-0.297$ (0.58), final $-0.402$ against $-0.411$ (0.20), mean $-0.417$
against $-0.470$ (1.60), with at most two of six seeds favouring it. The smallest
detectable difference was 0.049 on a range of 1.082. The larger roster is nominally worse
on all three measures, but this does not reach significance.

\subsection{Ensembles of independently trained policies}
A majority vote over six independently trained phase-relational checkpoints matches its
best member ($-0.12$) and exceeds the member mean ($-0.172$, 2.20 standard errors), so its
benefit is in selection rather than magnitude. Averaging the weights of the same
checkpoints fails ($-3.87$), since independently seeded runs occupy different loss
basins~\cite{permsym}; this does not apply to federated averaging, whose clients start
each round from a common point. These results are based on a single group of six.

\subsection{Downstream coverage and FRAP}\label{sec:frapcov}
FRAP transfers well but performs poorly on the training networks. Its state is
per-movement pressure, which reduces to queue length where a movement has no downstream
lane in the modelled area. The fraction of movements with a downstream lane follows its
results across the four networks: 75\% on \texttt{grid4x4} and \texttt{arterial4x4},
where it performs well, against 39\% on \texttt{ingolstadt7} and 33\% on
\texttt{cologne3}, where it performs worst. Braun~\cite{graphiface} reports a similar
sensitivity to signal coverage. This is a supported hypothesis; the isolating experiment
has not been performed.

\subsection{Evaluation artifacts}\label{sec:artifacts}
Six artifacts produced plausible but incorrect numbers during this study.
(1)~\emph{Silent holdout substitution}: when a roster's output width is narrower than the
holdout's action space, the evaluator falls back to a training city without warning.
(2)~\emph{Holdout topology leakage}: a roster contained a training city whose network
file was identical to the holdout's. (3)~\emph{Untrained output rows}, as in
Section~\ref{sec:problem}. (4)~\emph{Scenario configuration drift}: route files,
evaluation windows and yellow interval differed from the benchmark's. (5)~\emph{A missing
signal phase}: a vendored network lacked one green phase present in the benchmark's copy,
which qualifies the \texttt{ingolstadt7} rows of Table~\ref{tab:indist}.
(6)~\emph{Survivorship in delay}: delay and trip time over arrived vehicles improve when
traffic is stranded. Artifacts 1, 2 and 6 arise from standard evaluation code.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}
The results identify positional indexing as the main obstacle to cross-topology transfer
in this setting and show that a configuration-free, phase-relational readout removes it.
The readout itself belongs to an existing family of phase-invariant
designs~\cite{frap,attendlight}; the contributions are the control experiment, the
configuration-free descriptor, and the re-evaluation of earlier results. Several
limitations remain. No comparison with TransferLight~\cite{transferlight} has been made.
Evaluation uses one simulator and four real networks, with training budgets shorter than
published practice. The policy improves waiting time, not average delay, relative to max
pressure, and in-distribution it does not outperform max pressure on every network. The
diversity result concerns topological diversity only. The FRAP arm is evaluated at a
five-round checkpoint and does not characterise MPLight as published.

% =============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
Positional indexing of a shared $Q$-head prevents transfer across intersection
topologies in the setting studied. The evidence consists of a control that excludes the
untrained-row explanation, a separation that persists under a fourfold budget increase,
reproduction with an independent phase-invariant readout, and the reversal of the two
strongest earlier interventions once the readout is corrected. A phase-relational readout
with descriptors derived from the simulator requires no per-intersection configuration,
keeps the network's signal program, transfers zero-shot to an unseen network, and
outperforms the released implementation of a concurrent method. Negative results obtained
with a positionally indexed readout in this setting should be re-examined before being
cited as evidence about the mechanisms they tested.
