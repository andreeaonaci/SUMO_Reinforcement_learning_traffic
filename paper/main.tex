\title{Phase-Relational $Q$-Learning: Configuration-Free\\
Traffic Signal Control Across Heterogeneous\\Intersection Topologies}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Author Name}
\IEEEauthorblockA{\textit{Department}\\\textit{Institution}\\City, Country\\email@domain}}

\begin{abstract}
A single reinforcement learning policy controlling intersections of differing
geometry must emit a fixed-width action vector, and the prevailing construction
assigns output row $k$ to phase index $k$, padding to a common maximum and masking
the remainder. This paper shows the convention is not a neutral encoding but the
binding constraint on cross-topology generalization: because phase orderings are
authored independently per network, row $k$ accumulates gradients from
semantically unrelated movements, index~1 denoting a protected left turn in two of
the benchmark networks used here and a through movement in the other two. A
control experiment separates this from the obvious alternative. On a holdout whose
phase count does not exceed the training maximum---so every selectable row is
fully trained---the indexed head still fails, scoring $-9028.88$ against
$-9296.84$ with untrained rows present, a difference within seed noise. A
phase-relational readout is proposed, evaluating $Q$ as a learned function of a
state embedding concatenated with a ten-dimensional descriptor of what each
candidate phase does, obtained automatically from the simulator's connection
table; parameter count becomes independent of action-space width and no
per-intersection configuration is required, unlike the phase-pair tables prior
phase-invariant methods depend upon. Across five configurations at six seeds each
it exceeds the indexed head by four orders of magnitude zero-shot on an unseen
network, and attains $21.4$\,s average delay at $91.6\%$ trip completion on the
RESCO Cologne corridor against $22.13$\,s and $23.99$\,s for published IPPO and
IDQN, whose completion rates are unreported and whose margin therefore cannot be
adjudicated. Thirty prior interventions
are inventoried; twenty-one are null, and the two strongest positives reverse once
the readout is corrected, establishing that the corpus measured a representational
floor.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Reinforcement learning controllers for traffic signals reliably outperform
fixed-time and actuated control on the network they were trained
on~\cite{intellilight,presslight,colight}. Deployment,
however, is economically interesting only if a controller trained on a handful of
instrumented intersections can be installed where it has never been. That
capability---zero-shot transfer across intersection \emph{topology}, as distinct
from transfer across demand on a fixed topology---remains open, and is the subject
of this paper.

Transfer across topology imposes a structural requirement absent from the
single-scenario case. Intersections differ in how many signal phases they possess;
in the benchmark networks used here the count ranges from two to eight. A shared
policy must therefore emit a fixed-width vector, and the standard construction pads
every intersection's action space to a common maximum $A_{\max}$ and supplies a
binary mask marking which rows are legal at the current intersection. Output row
$k$ is thereby bound to ``phase index $k$'' at whichever intersection is being
controlled.

The convention is routine enough that it is rarely stated as a modelling decision.
It is nonetheless a strong one. Phase orderings inside a SUMO \texttt{tlLogic}
block are authored per network by whoever built it and carry no cross-network
meaning. Row $k$ is consequently trained against whatever movements happen to
occupy slot $k$ in each contributing network, and those differ. The central
empirical claim of this paper is that this collision---not network capacity, not
the learning algorithm, not the aggregation rule, and not the training
budget---prevents transfer.

The claim is not that phase-invariant readouts are new. FRAP~\cite{frap} introduced
pairwise phase competition in 2019 and reported transfer without retraining, and
AttendLight~\cite{attendlight} handles arbitrary phase counts through attention.
The contributions here are of a different kind.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{An isolating control} (Section~\ref{sec:control}) that removes the
      untrained-row confound and shows the failure survives its removal,
      separating ``the representation is wrong'' from ``the representation is
      undertrained''---which admit different remedies.
\item \textbf{A configuration-free readout} (Section~\ref{sec:method}) whose phase
      descriptor is derived from the simulator's connection table, removing the
      hand-authored per-signal movement tables FRAP and MPLight require before
      they can be applied at all.
\item \textbf{A reinterpretation of a body of negative results}
      (Section~\ref{sec:inventory}): thirty interventions previously evaluated in
      this setting, with the two strongest re-run on the corrected readout to show
      the corpus was measuring a floor.
\item \textbf{Six evaluation artifacts} (Section~\ref{sec:artifacts}), each of
      which produced a plausible but incorrect publication-style number, several
      arising from tooling in common use.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Reinforcement learning for traffic signals}
Deep $Q$-learning was applied to isolated intersections
by IntelliLight~\cite{intellilight}, after which the field moved to
coordination~\cite{presslight,colight}; a survey~\cite{survey} observes that gains
are usually reported on the network a method was trained on. RESCO's benchmark
study~\cite{resco} finds several published methods underperform well-tuned
heuristics under realistic demand, which Section~\ref{sec:inventory} supports from
a different direction. The trunk here follows standard
practice~\cite{dqn,ddqn,dueling}, the readout rather than the learner being the
object of study.

\subsection{Phase representation}
FRAP~\cite{frap} represents a phase as a pair of competing movements and scores
all ordered pairs through a competition mask, yielding invariance to flip and
rotation. MPLight~\cite{mplight} adopts FRAP as backbone and supplies pressure as
both state and reward, demonstrating scale to thousands of signals.
AttendLight~\cite{attendlight} applies attention over phases and is explicit that
universality holds ``as long as a similar configuration is represented in the
training set.''

The property that proves decisive here is not phase-invariance but
\emph{configuration-freedom}. FRAP and MPLight require, per scenario and per
signal, a hand-authored table mapping local phase indices onto a global movement
vocabulary; the reference benchmark's documentation states that movements ``must
be remapped to correspond to the same action in each agent,'' and its
\texttt{cologne3} configuration supplies three such dictionaries written by hand.
An intersection for which nobody has written that table cannot be controlled at
all. The descriptor of Section~\ref{sec:method}-B is derived from the simulator's
connection table at run time, which is what distinguishes it. That difference is a
capability rather than a performance claim, and is treated as such throughout.

\subsection{Transfer and generalization}
MetaLight~\cite{metalight} adapts to a new intersection by meta-learning, which
presumes a few gradient steps on the target are available; the setting here is
strictly zero-shot. X-Light~\cite{xlight} learns across cities with a hierarchical
transformer, and inductive graph formulations~\cite{igrl} and
TransferLight~\cite{transferlight} target deployment on unseen networks, the
latter crediting domain randomization over randomly generated road networks.

Concurrent with this work, Braun~\cite{graphiface} scores individual movements
with a shared graph network and converts those scores into each junction's
variable-sized phase set through a deterministic incidence matrix, so that
parameter shapes are likewise independent of junction action count. The
convergence is notable because the constructions were arrived at separately and
differ in almost every particular---a policy-gradient learner over a movement
graph there against a value-based per-phase descriptor here---while agreeing that
the action interface, not the learner, is what must be made topology-independent.
That work reports sensitivity to a signal-coverage distribution shift, which is
independent corroboration of the coverage account this paper gives for FRAP in
Section~\ref{sec:results}-D.

Neither the phase-invariant readout, nor zero-shot cross-topology transfer, nor
the federated setting is claimed as novel here. What is claimed is the control of
Section~\ref{sec:control}, which asks a question about the \emph{indexed} readout
that none of these works had occasion to ask, each having replaced it rather than
characterised it---so the cost of retaining it remained unmeasured.

\subsection{Federated and curriculum training}
Aggregation follows FedAvg~\cite{fedavg}; FedProx~\cite{fedprox} and a
Reptile-style meta-aggregation~\cite{reptile} appear as arms in
Table~\ref{tab:inventory}. Federated formulations for signal control~\cite{hfrl}
address privacy and scale, including hierarchical variants that cluster
intersections before averaging. Ordered client curricula in federated learning
were studied by Vahidian \emph{et al.}~\cite{curricula}, whose ordering,
anti-ordering and random-ordering comparison the curriculum arms of
Table~\ref{tab:curriculum} follow; that construction is prior art and is not
claimed here, and the contribution of Table~\ref{tab:curriculum} is instead the
observation that it \emph{reverses} once the readout is corrected.

\section{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:problem}

Let $\mathcal{C}$ be a set of road networks. Network $c\in\mathcal{C}$ contains
signalised intersections $i\in\mathcal{I}_c$. Each intersection is observed every
$\Delta t=5$\,s with local observation $o_i$, action set $\mathcal{A}_i$ of size
$A_i$ equal to its green-phase count, and reward $r_i$ given by the negative change
in cumulative waiting time over its incoming lanes.

A shared policy is one parameter vector $\theta$ used at every intersection of every
network. Since $A_i$ varies, $\theta$ must induce a function of common output width
$A_{\max}=\max_i A_i$ with mask $m_i\in\{0,1\}^{A_{\max}}$, $m_i[k]=1$ iff $k<A_i$:
\begin{equation}
a_i \;=\; \arg\max_{k\,:\,m_i[k]=1}\; Q_\theta(o_i)[k].
\label{eq:argmax}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Positional indexing and its semantics}
Write $\sigma_i(k)$ for the set of traffic movements phase $k$ serves at
intersection $i$---the physical meaning of that action. The standard readout is
\begin{equation}
Q_\theta(o_i) \;=\; W\,h_\theta(o_i) + b,
\qquad W\in\mathbb{R}^{A_{\max}\times d},
\label{eq:indexed}
\end{equation}
so row $W_k$ alone values action $k$ everywhere and is trained on
$\bigcup_i \sigma_i(k)$ over all training intersections. Transfer to an unseen
intersection $j$ requires $W_k$ to value $\sigma_j(k)$, yet nothing in
\eqref{eq:indexed} ties $\sigma_j(k)$ to any $\sigma_i(k)$ seen in training. Two
failure modes follow. \emph{Untrained rows}: if $A_j>\max_i A_i$, rows
$k\ge\max_i A_i$ were never updated, and on the rosters used here $37.5\%$ of the
holdout's legal action space is scored by such rows. \emph{Semantic collision}:
even within the trained range $\sigma_i(k)$ disagrees across $i$---index~1 denotes
a protected left turn in \texttt{arterial4x4} and \texttt{grid4x4} and a through
movement in \texttt{cologne3} and \texttt{ingolstadt7} (Fig.~\ref{fig:collision}).

Which dominates is the empirical question Section~\ref{sec:control} answers. It
matters because the first admits a cheap remedy---widen the head, or add a
training city with more phases---and the second does not.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Shared observation contract}
Every intersection in every network emits a tuple of identical shape
(Fig.~\ref{fig:obs}). Topology enters only through the numerical content of these
tensors; nothing downstream branches on city or intersection type, and the policy
never receives a city identifier.

\subsection{Phase descriptors}
For candidate phase $a$ at intersection $i$, let $L_i(a)$ be the incoming lanes
that $a$ gives right of way, obtained by intersecting the phase's state string with
the controlled-links table, and $L^{\mathrm{out}}_i(a)$ the downstream lanes those
movements discharge onto. The descriptor $\phi_i(a)\in\mathbb{R}^{10}$ collects, in
order: phase pressure
$\mathrm{clip}\big((q_{\mathrm{in}}-q_{\mathrm{out}})/10,\,\pm5\big)$; normalised
queue; normalised accumulated waiting time; mean occupancy; lane count; an
indicator that $a$ is the phase currently displayed; time elapsed in that phase if
so; and the fractions of greened lanes whose movement is straight, left and right.

Two properties matter. First, every entry is computable at any signalised
intersection from the simulator alone, so there is no per-intersection
configuration to author or to omit. Second, the final three entries describe what a
phase \emph{does} in a vocabulary that recurs across networks: ``a phase serving
mostly left turns, with a long queue and positive pressure'' is a description that
remains meaningful at an intersection never seen in training. This is the property
positional indices lack.

\subsection{Phase-relational $Q$-function}
Let $h_\theta(o_i)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$, $d=128$, be the state embedding from the
shared trunk: a lane encoder, a neighbour encoder with hop embeddings, masked
multi-head attention using the neighbour mask as key-padding mask, and a fusion
MLP. The proposed readout replaces \eqref{eq:indexed} with
\begin{equation}
Q_\theta(o_i)[a] \;=\; f_\psi\!\big([\,h_\theta(o_i)\,\|\,\phi_i(a)\,]\big),
\qquad f_\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d+10}\!\rightarrow\!\mathbb{R},
\label{eq:phaserel}
\end{equation}
with $f_\psi$ a three-layer MLP applied independently to each candidate phase. The
state embedding is computed once per tick and broadcast across phases, so the cost
is one trunk evaluation plus $A_i$ scorer evaluations.

Equation~\eqref{eq:phaserel} has no per-action parameters, from which four
consequences follow. $|\psi|$ is independent of $A_{\max}$, so widening the action
space adds no parameters and creates no rows training can fail to reach; a
three-phase intersection trains exactly the parameters an eight-phase intersection
uses, eliminating the untrained-row mode by construction; there are no indices
whose meaning can conflict, eliminating the collision of Fig.~\ref{fig:collision};
and $\max_a Q$ is computable for any $A_i$, including counts never seen in
training. A fifth consequence is representational: because $\phi_i(a)$ contains
per-phase pressure, the max-pressure rule~\cite{varaiya} lies inside the
hypothesis space of \eqref{eq:phaserel}, whereas under \eqref{eq:indexed} the
observation carried only an intersection-level pressure scalar and that rule was
not representable at any capacity. The earlier formulation was thus not merely
harder to optimise but strictly less expressive. Fig.~\ref{fig:readouts} contrasts
the three readouts.

\subsection{Federated training}
Cities train in parallel worker processes, each retaining a warm simulator, replay
buffer and optimizer state across rounds; only parameter tensors cross process
boundaries. Each round broadcasts $\theta$, trains each city for $E$ local
episodes, and aggregates by sample-count weighting. Under the indexed readout,
aggregation additionally averages row $k$ only over cities that exercised $k$;
readouts B and C have no per-action rows, so this reduces to plain averaging. The
aggregate is evaluated after every round on a network excluded from training.

\subsection{Signal timing and which results are externally comparable}
The yellow interval is not uniform across this study, and the distinction governs
which tables may be set beside published numbers. The simulator library's default
is $2$\,s; the benchmark specifies $3$\,s. At a $5$\,s action interval a $2$\,s
yellow leaves $3$\,s of usable green per phase change against the benchmark's
$2$\,s, i.e.\ $50\%$ more, for \emph{every} controller. Comparing a $2$\,s-yellow
measurement with a published $3$\,s-yellow number is therefore invalid, and doing
so produced a claim that had to be withdrawn earlier in this study.

Three protocol constraints are enforced throughout. The $\varepsilon$ decay is
sized from the total round count, so arms of a comparison must use identical round
counts---a detail that invalidated one earlier comparison---and all do. Average
delay and trip time are computed over \emph{arrived} vehicles, so a controller
that strands traffic reports better values; every delay figure is therefore given
with its completion rate against the departure total of
Table~\ref{tab:networks}. Results are over six seeds unless stated, with
significance $|\Delta|/\mathrm{SE}$ unpaired on population standard deviation at a
threshold of $2$, drop-one influence reported so a result carried by one seed is
visible, and three-seed outcomes labelled screens rather than
confirmations---a policy adopted after five interventions produced clean,
unanimous three-seed results that reversed or evaporated at six.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The representation, not undertrained rows}\label{sec:control}
Section~\ref{sec:problem} identified two candidate failure modes. They separate by
replacing the holdout with a network whose phase count does not exceed the training
maximum, so every row the holdout can select has been trained by every training
city. Were untrained rows the cause, performance should recover.

the readout fails even when fully trained. This is the paper's central control, and
it licenses the interpretation of everything that follows. Note also what it rules
out on the remedy side---widening the head or adding a high-phase-count training
city, the two cheap fixes suggested by the untrained-row account, cannot work.

favouring it on every measure and $|\Delta|/\mathrm{SE}$ between $23.6$ and
$96.7$. The two run at the benchmark's own $3$\,s yellow are reported first, being
the ones that may be set beside published work.

comparison carrying no such qualification, the proposed readout having the highest
completion rate in that table and still being behind on delay. The honest summary:
competitive with published learned baselines in-distribution, not competitive with
a well-tuned heuristic on every network.

\subsection{Why FRAP degrades in-distribution}
FRAP transfers well yet performs poorly on the training networks. Its entire state
is per-movement pressure, inbound minus downstream queue, which degenerates to
plain queue length wherever a movement has no downstream lane inside the modelled
area. Coverage computed from the benchmark's own wiring tracks its results across
all four networks: $75\%$ in \texttt{grid4x4} and \texttt{arterial4x4}, where FRAP
is strong, against $39\%$ in \texttt{ingolstadt7} and $33\%$ in \texttt{cologne3},
where it is weak and weakest. Corridor networks, in which most movements discharge
off-map, deprive a pressure-only state of its signal; regular grids retain it.
This is a property of the state design rather than the port, pressure being
computed as the reference implementation computes it. It is offered as a supported
hypothesis, not a demonstrated mechanism: four networks, confounded with budget,
and the isolating experiment has not been run.

\section{The Experimental Programme}\label{sec:programme}

The control of Section~\ref{sec:control} was the study's conclusion, not its
starting point. Because Section~\ref{sec:inventory} argues the preceding
experiments were measuring a floor, they are summarised here in enough detail for
that argument to be checked.

\subsection{Characterising the failure: confident lock-in}
The earliest symptom was bimodality rather than poor average performance:
re-evaluating a collapsed checkpoint over thirty \emph{different} simulator seeds
returned rewards clustered onto a handful of values---in the extreme case a
standard deviation of exactly $0.00$---and within a checkpoint possessing genuine
variance the mean gap between best and second-best $Q$-value correlated $-0.884$
with episode reward, so the more confident the network the worse it performed.
Weight divergence between cities did not predict which round would collapse, the
collapses survived a sixfold increase in evaluation episodes, and removing
federation did not remove the phenomenon ($7/100$ model-rounds federated against
$12/200$ independent, $0.34$); the seven interventions aimed at the mechanism are
rows of Table~\ref{tab:inventory}. In hindsight a readout whose rows carry
contradictory meanings across cities predicts exactly this signature: confident
commitment to an action whose value was averaged over incompatible targets.

\subsection{Budget, diversity, and adaptation}
\emph{Budget.} Published methods on these scenarios train for roughly one hundred
episodes per scenario against this study's forty. In-distribution at an extended
budget the best checkpoint reached $37.4$\,s mean waiting time, $6.2\times$ better
than fixed time and within $1.4\times$ of max pressure---budget was almost the
entire story there. Under the cross-topology protocol the same extension improved
the holdout significantly ($2.38/2.43$) yet left a gap of several thousand-fold,
so the two comparisons are not interchangeable, and conflating them produced an
overstated claim that was withdrawn. Table~\ref{tab:zeroshot} settles the question
for the readout: at $2.5$--$4\times$ budget the indexed head remains four orders of
magnitude behind, the final-round gap \emph{wider} than the best-round gap, where
undertraining predicts the opposite.

\emph{Diversity.} Eight synthetic irregular grids, generated by removing interior
signalised junctions from a lattice, gave fourteen training cities and $116$
intersections against the three-city roster's $26$. The result was null
($-6304.85$ against $-6110.73$, $0.60$). Two generator defaults had to be
overridden or the test would have been vacuous---SUMO's default signal layout
collapses every generated junction to two phases regardless of geometry, and at
single-lane width it does not signalise junctions at all---and a third defect
surfaced later: the roster contained a training city whose network file was
identical to the holdout's (artifact~2), so the experiment did not measure
cross-topology generalization at all. The null cannot be relied on in either
direction, and a corrected repetition on the phase-relational readout was
inconclusive because the holdout was saturated.

\emph{Test-time adaptation.} Fine-tuning on \emph{synthetic} randomised demand over
the holdout topology---never the evaluation route file---was the corpus's largest
effect: against a zero-shot baseline of $-8664.73$ ($\mathrm{std}=0.00$ over thirty
episodes) the adapted checkpoint scored $-1092.10$, $|\Delta|/\mathrm{SE}=72.78$
over four seeds, every episode beating the zero-shot constant. It did not survive
its control: a randomly initialised arm---same architecture and procedure,
pretrained weights discarded---scored $-406.85$ against the pretrained arm's
$-693.84$. \emph{Training on the target topology helps; federated pre-training does
not transfer.} The same runs showed the federated model's final checkpoint to be
worse on the holdout than a random network, and a two-phase learning-rate schedule
failed to arrest the degradation, arguing the instability is not a step-size
problem. Section~\ref{sec:inventory}-A reports what happens when this intervention
is repeated on the corrected readout.

\emph{Evaluation-time combination.} A majority vote over six independently trained
checkpoints beat every member and their weight-space average~\cite{swa} under the
indexed readout. Repeated on the corrected readout it splits: the vote beats the
member mean by $2.20$ times the member-level standard error but only \emph{matches}
its best member, so its value is selection---which seed is best is not identifiable
without a holdout---while the weight-space average reverses outright to an order of
magnitude below the worst member, independently seeded runs occupying distinct loss
basins where parameter averaging is defined only up to permutation
symmetry~\cite{permsym}. This does not bear on federated averaging, whose clients
are broadcast from a common point each round and never leave a shared basin. Both
rest on one group of six and are screens.

\subsection{Scenario design}
Three properties of the evaluation had to be established, each costing a discarded
scenario. \emph{Saturation:} the original holdout was uncongested, both baselines
clearing $99\%$ of vehicles. Re-testing at triple demand took two attempts---a file
assumed heavy from its name gave $0.00$\,s waiting for both baselines, a ceiling,
and a candidate at $8.5\times$ per-signal load gridlocked max pressure itself, a
floor. Difficulty must be calibrated by the baseline controller's own waiting time,
not by vehicles per signal, which does not normalise across networks of differing
capacity. At correctly calibrated triple demand the proposed readout scored $-0.32$
against max pressure's $-0.640$ and fixed time's $-170.51$ at matched throughput,
with fixed time degrading $62\times$---so neither baseline was coasting.
\emph{Topological novelty:} a holdout must not share a network with a training
city, which one roster silently did (artifact~2). \emph{Untrained rows:} on the
four-phase holdout built for the control of Section~\ref{sec:control} the proposed
readout scored $-1.38$ against max pressure's $-2.225$ at matched throughput while
the indexed readout gridlocked at $-9107.61$, losing even to fixed time.

Five explanations for the cross-topology gap were therefore tested and rejected
before the sixth was confirmed: optimiser instability, training budget, data
diversity, the aggregation rule, and network capacity. The representation was
tested last because it was the assumption least visible as an assumption---it is
inherited from single-scenario practice, where it is harmless.

\section{The Intervention Corpus}\label{sec:inventory}

Before the readout was identified as the constraint, thirty interventions were
evaluated under the indexed readout on the same rosters and the same criterion.
Table~\ref{tab:inventory} is the complete inventory, presented not as a record of
effort but as evidence: the shape of the distribution is the argument.

Twenty-one were null or negative, four inconclusive, and five reached threshold.
Of those five, three are test-time procedures that do not alter the learned
policy, and a fourth---fine-tuning on the target topology---was matched by a
randomly initialised control and then reversed outright on the corrected readout.
\textbf{No training-time modification to network architecture, learning algorithm,
aggregation rule or loss produced a durable improvement.} This is not the expected
outcome of thirty independent hypotheses: aggregation rules, architectural
capacity, algorithm family and loss shaping are largely orthogonal mechanisms, and
that all return null on the same task is characteristic of a shared constraint
upstream of all of them.

\emph{Aggregation} (ten entries) is the most complete sweep---five weighting
schemes, clustering by action width, a proximal term~\cite{fedprox}, server
momentum, meta-aggregation~\cite{reptile}, and the ablation of federation
entirely, which is statistically indistinguishable from federated training
($1.78/1.43$). Since the object being averaged is a readout that cannot express a
transferable policy, no averaging rule over it can recover one.
\emph{Architecture} (eleven entries) spans capacity, depth, attention width,
normalization, recurrence, topology conditioning, bootstrapped heads and low-rank
adapters; the instructive pair is a differential learning rate slowing the trunk,
negative at $2.15/1.99$, against a zero-initialised low-rank adapter on a normally
trained trunk, null at $0.38/0.28$. Starving the representation hurts, adding
capacity to it does not help---the asymmetry expected when the bottleneck sits
downstream of the representation. \emph{Algorithm} (five entries) substitutes the
learner wholesale~\cite{ppo,munchausen,qrdqn,maml} without effect, which is
difficult to reconcile with any account in which the difficulty is one of credit
assignment or exploration. \emph{Loss shaping} (five entries) contains the
corpus's one training-time confirmation, potential-based
shaping~\cite{potential} on the max-pressure potential ($\approx2.5$), modest and
not altering the ordering of methods.

\subsection{The two strongest positives reverse}
A progressive curriculum ordering cities by complexity, warming up on the simplest
and fine-tuning each newly admitted city before pooling it---the ordered-client
construction of~\cite{curricula}, which is prior art and not claimed here---had
been confirmed at six seeds under the indexed readout ($2.42/3.42$). Re-running it
on the corrected readout inverts the result (Table~\ref{tab:curriculum}). Plain
averaging beats both curriculum arms on all six seeds, and the ordering itself is
a null ($1.99$) with the reverse ordering nominally superior. Since the
intervention did not change, what it was measuring must have: under the indexed
readout it was recovering part of a representational deficit rather than
contributing a training-procedure benefit.

gives $-0.253$ and two rounds $-0.227$, at $|\Delta|/\mathrm{SE}$ of $5.40$ and

The mechanism follows the curriculum's logic. Under the indexed readout the
zero-shot policy was gridlocked at $-8664.73$, so nearly any adaptation improved
it, and what was measured was the size of the representational deficit rather than
the value of adaptation. On the corrected readout the zero-shot policy already
beats max pressure, so a short burst on synthetic demand can only displace a
near-optimal policy---the prediction of the randomly initialised control carried
to its conclusion: once pre-training genuinely transfers, target adaptation stops
helping. The floor-effect reinterpretation therefore rests on two measured
reversals, the second being the largest effect in the inventory. A narrower
reading is equally consistent and is not excluded---that adapting to
\emph{synthetic} demand harms an already near-optimal policy, carrying no
implication for real target traffic---and three seeds is a screen.

\section{Evaluation Artifacts}\label{sec:artifacts}

Six artifacts were encountered, each producing a plausible but incorrect number,
reported because several arise from tooling in common use rather than from this
implementation. (1)~\emph{Silent holdout substitution}: where a reduced roster's
output width is narrower than the holdout's action space, the evaluator falls back
to a training city without raising, yielding an in-distribution measurement
labelled cross-topology. (2)~\emph{Holdout topology leakage}: a roster contained a
training city whose network file was identical to the holdout's, differing only in
demand---name-based holdout checks do not detect this.
(3)~\emph{Untrained output rows}: $37.5\%$ of a holdout's action space scored by
rows no training city exercised. (4)~\emph{Scenario configuration drift}: route
files, evaluation windows and yellow interval all differed from the benchmark's, a
$2$\,s rather than $3$\,s yellow granting every controller $50\%$ more usable green
per phase change at a $5$\,s action interval. (5)~\emph{A missing signal phase}: a
vendored network omitted one green phase present in the benchmark's copy, reducing
one intersection's action space and qualifying Table~\ref{tab:indist}.
(6)~\emph{Survivorship in delay metrics}: delay and trip time are computed over
arrived vehicles, so stranding traffic improves them, which inverted one comparison
before throughput was reported alongside.

Artifacts 1, 2 and 6 are properties of standard evaluation code, and each is
capable of producing a publishable-looking result in the wrong direction.

\section{Limitations}

The proposed readout is not a novel architecture; phase-invariant readouts predate
it~\cite{frap,attendlight}, and the contributions rest on the control of
Section~\ref{sec:control}, the configuration-freedom of
Section~\ref{sec:method}-B and the reinterpretation of
Section~\ref{sec:inventory}. No comparison against a contemporary zero-shot method
has been performed, so competitiveness with the recent state of the art is
unestablished; TransferLight~\cite{transferlight} is the obvious comparator and
uses overlapping scenarios. Evaluation is confined to one simulator and four
networks, and training budgets are short relative to published practice. An
attempt to establish whether training-topology diversity improves robustness was
inconclusive because the holdout was saturated, and is reported as a failed
measurement rather than a null. The FRAP arm is evaluated at a five-round
checkpoint and its in-distribution figures should not be read as characterising
MPLight as published.

\section{Conclusion}

Positional indexing of a shared $Q$-head is the binding constraint on
cross-topology generalization in this setting. The claim rests on a control that
eliminates the untrained-row explanation, on persistence of the separation under a
fourfold budget increase, on independent reproduction through a published
phase-invariant readout, and on reversal of the two strongest prior interventions
once the readout is corrected. Replacing the readout with a phase-relational form
whose descriptors are derived from the simulator removes the per-intersection
configuration comparable methods require and recovers most of the gap.

The consequence for practice is narrow and specific. Negative results obtained in
this setting through a positionally indexed readout do not constitute evidence
about the mechanisms they were designed to test, and should be re-examined before
being cited as such.
