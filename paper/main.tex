\begin{abstract}
One reinforcement learning policy that controls traffic signals on networks it has never
seen needs actions that mean the same thing on every network. The usual design gives each
policy output a fixed phase number and masks the outputs that an intersection does not
use. Each network numbers its phases differently, so one output is trained on unrelated
movements. A control experiment shows that this numbering, and not undertrained outputs,
causes the failure. We replace phase numbers with an output head that scores each phase
from ten features of the lanes it serves, such as queue length, waiting time and turn
direction. The simulator provides these features automatically, so no per-intersection
configuration is needed and each network keeps its own signal plan. We train with
federated learning on three RESCO benchmark networks and test without retraining on four
unseen networks. On the main test network the new head completes 98.9\% of trips, while
phase numbering gridlocks at 16--20\%. On that network it has 18\% lower waiting time than
max pressure at similar delay and completion, and 7.1 times lower average delay than a
recent graph-based transfer method (2.4 to 7.1 times across three networks). On two further
Cologne networks it has 32--44\% lower delay than fixed-time control at similar completion
and matches max pressure on the eight-intersection one, but it is worse than both on a
21-intersection network. On a training network it matches the published delay of RESCO's
IPPO and IDQN at 91.6\% completion with one tenth of their training budget. The results
show that the phase representation, not undertraining, is the main barrier to zero-shot
transfer in traffic signal control.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Reinforcement learning (RL) controllers for traffic signals perform better than fixed-time
and actuated control on the networks they are trained on~\cite{intellilight,presslight,colight}.
For deployment, a more useful property is \emph{zero-shot transfer across topology}: a
controller trained on a few intersections should work at intersections it has never seen,
without retraining. This is different from transfer to new traffic demand on the same
network, and it is still an open problem.

The difficulty is the action space. Intersections have different numbers of signal phases
(from two to eight in the networks used here), but a shared policy has an output of fixed
width. The usual solution pads every action space to a common maximum $A_{\max}$ and masks
the actions an intersection does not have. Output $k$ then always means ``phase number
$k$'' of the current intersection. Phase numbers, however, are chosen by whoever built each
network and have no common meaning. Output $k$ is therefore trained on whatever movements
happen to be phase $k$ in each training network.

Earlier work has proposed outputs that do not depend on phase numbers~\cite{frap,mplight,attendlight},
and a concurrent method scores movements with a graph network~\cite{graphiface}. These works
do not measure why the phase-numbered output fails. They also need either a hand-written
phase-to-movement table for every intersection~\cite{frap,mplight} or they replace the
network's own signal program with generated phases~\cite{graphiface}.

This paper makes four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A control experiment (Section~\ref{sec:control}) that separates the two possible
      causes of failure of the phase-numbered output: untrained outputs, and outputs whose
      meaning differs between networks. The failure remains when every output that can be
      selected is fully trained.
\item A phase-relational output head (Section~\ref{sec:method}) that describes each phase
      with features read from the simulator. It needs no per-intersection configuration
      and keeps the network's own signal program.
\item An evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:results}) on four unseen networks at the RESCO
      benchmark's settings, against max pressure, fixed time, FRAP, the published RESCO
      results and the released code of the concurrent method.
\item A re-test of 41 earlier interventions (Section~\ref{sec:inventory}); the two
      strongest of them reverse once the output head is corrected.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Reinforcement learning for traffic signal control}
IntelliLight~\cite{intellilight} applied deep $Q$-learning to single intersections, and
later work added coordination between intersections~\cite{presslight,colight}. A
survey~\cite{survey} notes that results are usually reported on the network a method was
trained on. The RESCO benchmark~\cite{resco} reports that several published methods are
worse than well-tuned rule-based controllers under realistic demand. Our learner is a
standard DQN~\cite{dqn,ddqn,dueling}; this paper studies only the output head.

\subsection{Phase representation}
FRAP~\cite{frap} describes a phase as a pair of movements and compares all pairs of
phases; this makes it invariant to flips and rotations of the intersection.
MPLight~\cite{mplight} uses FRAP with pressure as state and reward and scales to thousands
of signals. AttendLight~\cite{attendlight} uses attention over lanes and phases and
generalizes when similar intersections appear in training. FRAP and MPLight need, for each
signal, a table that maps local phase numbers to a common set of movements. RESCO's
documentation says movements ``must be remapped to correspond to the same action in each
agent'', and its \texttt{cologne3} configuration contains three such hand-written tables.
Our phase features (Section~\ref{sec:descriptors}) are computed automatically instead.
This is a difference in what the method needs, not a claim of better performance.

\subsection{Transfer to new networks}
MetaLight~\cite{metalight} adapts to new intersections by meta-learning, so it needs
training steps on the target. X-Light~\cite{xlight} learns across cities with a
hierarchical transformer. Inductive graph methods~\cite{igrl} and
TransferLight~\cite{transferlight} target unseen networks; TransferLight trains on many
randomly generated road networks. Concurrently, Braun~\cite{graphiface} scores single
movements with a shared graph network and maps the scores to each junction's phases
through an incidence matrix, so the number of parameters does not depend on the number of
phases. That method is trained with policy gradients and generates its own phase sets; we
compare with it in Section~\ref{sec:braun}. We do not claim phase-invariant scoring,
zero-shot transfer or federated training as new. Our contributions are the control
experiment of Section~\ref{sec:control} and the configuration-free output head.

\subsection{Federated and curriculum training}
We aggregate with FedAvg~\cite{fedavg}. FedProx~\cite{fedprox} and Reptile-style
aggregation~\cite{reptile} are among the re-tested interventions. Federated traffic signal
control~\cite{hfrl} has been studied for privacy and scale, including variants that
cluster intersections before averaging. Vahidian \emph{et al.}~\cite{curricula} studied
ordered client curricula; our curriculum runs (Table~\ref{tab:curriculum}) follow their
construction.

\section{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:problem}

Let $\mathcal{C}$ be a set of road networks. Network $c\in\mathcal{C}$ has signalized
intersections $i\in\mathcal{I}_c$. Every $\Delta t=5$\,s, intersection $i$ receives an
observation $o_i$ and chooses one of its $A_i$ green phases. Its reward $r_i$ is the
negative change in total waiting time on its incoming lanes.

One parameter vector $\theta$ is used at every intersection of every network. Because $A_i$
differs between intersections, the output has a common width $A_{\max}=\max_i A_i$, and a
mask $m_i\in\{0,1\}^{A_{\max}}$ marks the real phases ($m_i[k]=1$ if and only if $k<A_i$).
The chosen phase is
\begin{equation}
a_i \;=\; \arg\max_{k\,:\,m_i[k]=1}\; Q_\theta(o_i)[k].
\label{eq:argmax}
\end{equation}

\subsection{The phase-numbered (indexed) head}
Let $\sigma_i(k)$ be the set of movements that phase $k$ serves at intersection $i$. The
usual output head is
\begin{equation}
Q_\theta(o_i) \;=\; W\,h_\theta(o_i) + b,
\qquad W\in\mathbb{R}^{A_{\max}\times d}.
\label{eq:indexed}
\end{equation}
Row $W_k$ alone gives the value of phase $k$ at every intersection, so it is trained on
the movements $\sigma_i(k)$ of all training intersections. At an unseen intersection $j$,
$W_k$ must value $\sigma_j(k)$, but nothing in \eqref{eq:indexed} links $\sigma_j(k)$ to
the movements seen in training. This can fail in two ways.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Untrained rows.} If $A_j$ is larger than every training intersection's phase
      count, the extra rows are never updated. On our main test network, $37.5\%$ of the
      legal actions are scored by such rows.
\item \emph{Conflicting meaning.} Even trained rows mean different movements in different
      networks. For example, phase~1 is a protected left turn in \texttt{arterial4x4} and
      \texttt{grid4x4}, but a through movement in \texttt{cologne3} and
      \texttt{ingolstadt7}. Row $W_1$ receives conflicting updates from these networks.
\end{itemize}
The first problem has easy fixes (a wider head, or a training network with more phases).
The second does not. Section~\ref{sec:control} tests which one causes the failure.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\input{figures/fig_arch.tex}
\caption{The complete system. (a)~At every intersection and every 5\,s, the
observation is read from SUMO: lane features sorted by queue, a summary of each neighbor
(with a validity mask; links are dropped at random to model unreliable communication),
ten features per candidate phase, and the mask of legal phases. The shared encoder turns
the lane and neighbor features into a state vector $h$. One shared scorer $f_\psi$ rates
every phase from $[h\,\|\,\phi(a)]$, and the best legal phase is applied. The encoder and
$f_\psi$ together form $\theta$; no parameter depends on the number of phases. (b)~In each
federated round, every worker keeps its own simulator, replay buffer and optimizer; only
$\theta$ and the sample count $n_c$ are exchanged.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure*}

Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the whole system. It has four parts: the observation, the phase
features, the output head and federated training.

\subsection{Observation}
Every intersection in every network produces an observation of the same shape:
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
o_i=\big(&\texttt{own}_{117},\,\texttt{nbr}_{8\times3},\,\texttt{nbrmask}_{8},\\
&\texttt{hop}_{8},\,m_{i,A_{\max}},\,\Phi_{i,A_{\max}\times10}\big).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
The own features are queue, waiting time, occupancy, speed and turn direction for up to
16 incoming lanes, plus five intersection-level values. Lanes are sorted by congestion, so
the model cannot learn the identity of a lane. Each of up to 8 neighbors contributes its
mean queue, mean waiting time and current phase. The model never receives a city or
intersection identifier, and no part of it depends on the intersection type.

\subsection{Phase features}\label{sec:descriptors}
For a candidate phase $a$ at intersection $i$, let $L_i(a)$ be the incoming lanes that get
green in $a$, read from the phase's signal state and the simulator's table of controlled
links, and let $L^{\mathrm{out}}_i(a)$ be the lanes those movements lead to. The feature
vector $\phi_i(a)\in\mathbb{R}^{10}$ contains:
(1)~pressure $\mathrm{clip}\big((q_{\mathrm{in}}-q_{\mathrm{out}})/10,\,\pm5\big)$;
(2)~normalized queue; (3)~normalized waiting time; (4)~mean occupancy; (5)~number of
lanes; (6)~whether $a$ is the current phase; (7)~how long it has been green if so; and
(8--10)~the fractions of its green lanes that go straight, left and right. All ten values
can be computed at any signalized intersection from the simulator alone, and the last
three describe what a phase does in terms that are the same in every network.

\subsection{Phase-relational output head}
Let $h_\theta(o_i)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$, $d=128$, be the state vector from the shared
encoder: a lane encoder, a neighbor encoder with hop-distance embedding, multi-head
attention that ignores masked neighbors, and a fusion MLP. Our head replaces
\eqref{eq:indexed} with
\begin{equation}
Q_\theta(o_i)[a] \;=\; f_\psi\!\big([\,h_\theta(o_i)\,\|\,\phi_i(a)\,]\big),
\qquad f_\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d+10}\!\rightarrow\!\mathbb{R},
\label{eq:phaserel}
\end{equation}
where $f_\psi$ is a three-layer MLP that scores each candidate phase with the same weights.
The encoder runs once per decision and $f_\psi$ runs once per phase.

The head has no parameters tied to an action number. This has three consequences. No
row can remain untrained, because the size of $\psi$ does not depend on $A_{\max}$. No
output number carries a meaning that can conflict between networks. And $\max_a Q$ is
defined for any number of phases, including numbers not seen in training. In addition,
$\phi_i(a)$ contains the pressure of each phase, so the head can represent the
max-pressure rule~\cite{varaiya}. The indexed head sees only one pressure value for the
whole intersection and cannot.

\subsection{Federated training}
Each training network runs in its own worker process, which keeps its simulator, replay
buffer and optimizer for the whole run. In each round, the server sends $\theta$ to all
workers; each worker trains its copy for $E$ episodes with Double DQN; and the server
averages the returned copies, weighted by their number of samples. For the indexed head,
row $k$ is averaged only over the networks that use it; our head has no such rows and uses
plain averaging. After every round, the averaged policy is tested on a network that is
never used for training.

\subsection{Signal timing}\label{sec:timing}
A controller chooses a phase every 5\,s. Choosing the current phase keeps it green. A
change inserts a yellow interval, and it is accepted only after the current phase has
lasted for the yellow interval plus a 5\,s minimum green. RESCO uses a 3\,s yellow, so the
shortest phase lasts 10\,s with 7\,s of green. Part of this study used SUMO's default of
2\,s, which gives every controller one more second of green per phase change. Results at
2\,s are therefore only compared with each other. Table~\ref{tab:config} gives the setting
of every result.

\subsection{Compared methods}
\emph{Phase-relational} (ours): the encoder of Section~\ref{sec:method} with head
\eqref{eq:phaserel}. \emph{Indexed}: the same encoder with head \eqref{eq:indexed}.
\emph{FRAP}: ported from the RESCO source code; its phase-comparison mask was checked to be
identical to the original, and it was given the hand-written phase tables of every
network, including the test network. \emph{Max pressure} and \emph{fixed time}: rule-based
controllers, run with the same evaluator and seeds. \emph{IPPO} and \emph{IDQN}: published
RESCO results~\cite{resco}. \emph{Braun}~\cite{graphiface}: released code, described in
Section~\ref{sec:braun}.

\subsection{Metrics and statistics}
\emph{Reward} is the training signal (negative change in total waiting time on incoming
lanes, averaged over the evaluation). It is always negative; closer to zero is better. We
use it only inside this study. \emph{Waiting time} is the time a vehicle spends below
0.1\,m/s. \emph{Delay} is the time lost compared with free-flow travel (SUMO
\texttt{timeLoss}), the main RESCO metric. \emph{Trip time} is the time from departure to
arrival. These three are averages over vehicles that arrived; lower is better. A
controller that leaves traffic stuck in the network therefore looks better on them. For
this reason we always give \emph{completion}, the share of vehicles departing in the
evaluation window that arrive, next to every delay value.

To test significance we use $|\Delta|/\mathrm{SE}$: the difference of the two means divided
by its standard error (unpaired, population standard deviation). A difference counts as
significant if $|\Delta|/\mathrm{SE}\ge 2$. We also check that no single seed decides the
result, and we call results with only three seeds \emph{screens}.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

The results answer six questions, one per subsection:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[A.] Why does the indexed head fail? (Section~\ref{sec:control})
\item[B.] Does the phase-relational head transfer to the unseen test network?
          (Section~\ref{sec:zeroshot})
\item[C.] Does it transfer to other unseen networks, and does a short fine-tune help?
          (Section~\ref{sec:unseen})
\item[D.] How does it perform on the training networks? (Section~\ref{sec:indist})
\item[E.] Is the gap caused by too little training, and how does it compare with FRAP?
          (Section~\ref{sec:budget})
\item[F.] How does it compare with the concurrent method of Braun? (Section~\ref{sec:braun})
\end{enumerate}
Table~\ref{tab:summary} gives the main numbers in one place. In short: the phase numbering
causes the failure; the phase-relational head transfers to every unseen network tested;
it has lower waiting time than max pressure where both complete the same traffic, but
the same average delay; it has lower delay than fixed time on the Cologne networks and
lower delay than Braun's method on all three networks compared; and it is worse than max
pressure on the 21-signal network in both delay and completion. It needs no
per-intersection configuration and about one tenth of RESCO's training budget per network.

\subsection{Does it transfer to other unseen networks?}\label{sec:unseen}
\emph{Test.} We evaluate the same final checkpoints, without further training, on three
RESCO networks that are not used in training. \texttt{cologne1} and \texttt{cologne8} share
no signal with \texttt{cologne3} (the nearest training signal is 685\,m and more than 4\,km
away). Of the 21 signals of \texttt{ingolstadt21}, 14 are new and 7 also appear in
\texttt{ingolstadt7}. We excluded \texttt{ingolstadt1} because its only signal is a training
signal. We checked overlap by signal identifier and by position. All three networks have
intersections with six phases, more than any training network. Each is evaluated in its
RESCO window at 3\,s yellow.

\emph{Result (zero-shot).} Table~\ref{tab:unseen} gives the results. On the two Cologne
networks the phase-relational head completes 97.8\% and 98.2\% of trips, against 62.1\% and
62.4\% for the indexed head. It has lower delay than fixed time on all three networks. On
\texttt{cologne8}, the largest fully unseen network, it ties max pressure on delay (27.5
against 27.8\,s, four of six seeds lower; seeds range from 25.7 to 28.9\,s) and has lower
waiting time on all six seeds (9.5 against 14.4\,s). On \texttt{cologne1} max pressure has
lower delay. \texttt{ingolstadt21} is the main weakness: the six seeds complete 70.6\% to
85.3\% of trips, less than max pressure (89.0\%) and fixed time (93.8\%). Four synthetic
irregular grids, also unseen, show the same pattern as \texttt{cologne1}: 96.0 to 97.6\%
completion for the phase-relational head, 7.4 to 12.3\% for the indexed head, and 3 to
17\,s lower delay for max pressure.

\emph{Result (fine-tuning).} We then fine-tuned each of the six checkpoints for one
episode on each network. Training uses synthetic traffic with the same total departure
rate as the evaluation hour; the evaluation routes are never used for training. No change
is significant (last row of Table~\ref{tab:unseen}). On \texttt{ingolstadt21}, where the
zero-shot policy is weakest, delay falls from 130.7 to 119.2\,s (five of six seeds) and
completion rises from 77.6 to 82.2\%, still below max pressure and fixed time. On
\texttt{cologne8} nothing changes. On \texttt{cologne1}, where the zero-shot policy is
already close to max pressure, fine-tuning makes it worse: four of six seeds get higher
delay, and one seed collapses to 108.5\,s and 63.0\% completion, which causes most of the
drop in the mean. Longer fine-tuning on \texttt{ingolstadt21} (three and ten episodes)
gives no further gain: delay 135.8 and 118.6\,s, completion 83.4 and 83.9\%, and no
difference from one episode exceeds $|\Delta|/\mathrm{SE}=1.05$. After ten episodes,
completion is 2.11 standard errors above zero-shot (five of six seeds), one of nine such
tests; every dose stays below max pressure and fixed time in completion.

\emph{Answer.} Yes on the two Cologne networks, where the head matches or beats the
rule-based controllers on most measures. No on \texttt{ingolstadt21}, where it is worse
than both rule-based controllers, and short fine-tuning does not close that gap.

\subsection{Is the gap caused by too little training? Comparison with FRAP}\label{sec:budget}

times the standard budget, on the \texttt{c1\_4\_6} roster at 2\,s yellow, and add FRAP
with the hand-written phase tables of every network (Table~\ref{tab:budget}).

\emph{Result.} The indexed head is still four orders of magnitude worse. Its gap at the
final round is larger than at its best round, which is the opposite of what undertraining

implementation: what matters is that the output is addressed by what a phase does, not
our specific features. The phase-relational head beats FRAP at the best round on all six

\emph{Answer.} No, more training does not close the gap. FRAP also transfers, but needs a
hand-written table per signal, and is less stable than the phase-relational head.

\subsection{Comparison with Braun}\label{sec:braun}
\emph{Test.} We ran the released code of Braun~\cite{graphiface}, at the commit cited in
that paper, on the same three training networks, and measured it with the same trip-level
evaluation as all other methods. We checked that our driver for its code gives exactly the
same results as its own evaluator. The method replaces a network's signal program with
every largest set of movements that do not conflict; on \texttt{grid4x4} this gives 17
phases instead of 8. We report two versions: the method as published (its own phases), and
the method restricted to the network's own phases. Its max-pressure controller on each phase
set checks whether differences come from the action space. Training uses the method's own
settings (PPO, 85 iterations, throughput reward, 10\,s decisions, 20\,s minimum green, 3\,s
yellow). This is 85 times the simulated time used to train our head. Each version uses six
seeds; evaluation samples actions, as in the method's own protocol.

\emph{Result.} Table~\ref{tab:braun}. The phase-relational head has lower delay on all
three networks ($|\Delta|/\mathrm{SE}$ of 46.1, 11.4 and 12.4 against the published
version). Braun's learned policy also has higher delay than Braun's own max-pressure
controller on every network and phase set, so the action space does not explain the
difference. The generated phases help max pressure on \texttt{grid4x4} (79.2 against
90.9\,s), hurt it on \texttt{cologne3} (67.7 against 26.4\,s), and cause gridlock on
\texttt{ingolstadt7} (44.1\% completion).

Three caveats apply. Braun's method optimizes throughput, and its completion is close to
that of the rule-based controllers (94.5 to 96.9\%). It was designed on its own networks.
And its published version uses a different action space, so it cannot be compared with
RESCO results. On \texttt{cologne3} it completes more trips than our head (96.9\% against
91.2\%).

\emph{Answer.} Our head has 2.4 to 7.1 times lower delay than Braun's method with 1/85 of
its simulated training time, at similar completion except on \texttt{cologne3}.

\section{Analysis}\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Re-test of earlier interventions}\label{sec:inventory}
Before we found the cause, we tested 41 interventions with the indexed head, on the same
rosters and with the same criterion (Table~\ref{tab:inventory}). Thirty-two were null or
negative, four were inconclusive and five were significant. Of the five, three are
test-time procedures that do not change the learned policy, and one (fine-tuning on the
test topology) was matched by a randomly initialized network given the same training. No
change to the architecture, learning algorithm, aggregation rule or loss gave a lasting
improvement. The interventions cover mostly unrelated mechanisms: ten aggregation variants,
including no federation at all (no different from federated training, 1.78/1.43); eleven
architecture variants; five learning algorithms~\cite{ppo,munchausen,qrdqn,maml}; and five
loss changes, of which only potential-based shaping~\cite{potential} was significant (about
2.5). When so many unrelated changes all fail, the limit is likely somewhere they all
share, which here is the output head.

internal), fine-tuning makes the policy worse: from $-0.123$ to $-0.250$ after one round
(4.44) and to $-0.205$ after two (3.75). Only one of twelve runs improved, by 0.02, and mean
waiting time rose from 0.29 to 0.67\,s. Across the six two-round runs, the correlation
between zero-shot reward and the change from fine-tuning is $r=-0.91$: fine-tuning helps
least where the starting policy is best. The one-episode fine-tune on the unseen real
networks (Table~\ref{tab:unseen}) shows the same order at 3\,s yellow.

Both reversals fit the view that these interventions were making up for the indexing
problem. Another explanation, that fine-tuning on synthetic traffic harms a policy that is
already close to optimal, cannot be ruled out.

\subsection{Training-topology diversity}\label{sec:diversity}
We added four synthetic irregular grids (a lattice with some interior signals removed) to
the three training networks and evaluated on the triple-demand test network. Over six
seeds, no measure favors the larger roster: best round $-0.283$ against $-0.297$ (0.58),
final $-0.402$ against $-0.411$ (0.20), mean $-0.417$ against $-0.470$ (1.60); at most two
of six seeds favor it. The smallest detectable difference was 0.049 on a range of 1.082.
The larger roster is slightly worse on all three measures, but not significantly.

\subsection{Ensembles of independently trained policies}
A majority vote over six independently trained phase-relational policies matches its best
member ($-0.12$) and beats the member mean ($-0.172$, 2.20 standard errors). Its benefit is
that it picks good actions without knowing which seed is best, not that it beats the best
seed. Averaging the weights of the same six policies fails ($-3.87$), because independently
seeded runs end up in different regions of the loss surface~\cite{permsym}. This does not
apply to federated averaging, whose workers start every round from the same parameters.
These results come from a single group of six seeds.

\subsection{Downstream coverage and FRAP}\label{sec:frapcov}
FRAP transfers well but performs poorly on the training networks. Its state is the pressure
of each movement, which reduces to the queue length when a movement has no downstream lane
in the modeled area. The share of movements with a downstream lane matches FRAP's results
on the four networks: 75\% on \texttt{grid4x4} and \texttt{arterial4x4}, where it does well,
and 39\% on \texttt{ingolstadt7} and 33\% on \texttt{cologne3}, where it does worst.
Braun~\cite{graphiface} reports a similar sensitivity to signal coverage. This explanation
is supported but not proven; we have not run the experiment that would isolate it.

\subsection{Evaluation errors found during the study}\label{sec:artifacts}
Six problems in the evaluation produced plausible but wrong numbers during this study.
(1)~\emph{Silent test-network swap}: when the output width was smaller than the test
network's action space, the evaluator silently used a training network instead.
(2)~\emph{Leaked test topology}: one roster contained a training network whose network
file was identical to the test network's. (3)~\emph{Untrained output rows}
(Section~\ref{sec:problem}). (4)~\emph{Settings drift}: route files, evaluation windows and
yellow interval differed from the benchmark's. (5)~\emph{A missing signal phase}: our copy
of a network lacked one green phase present in the benchmark's copy; this affects the
\texttt{ingolstadt7} rows of Table~\ref{tab:indist}. (6)~\emph{Stranded vehicles}: delay and
trip time, averaged over arrived vehicles, improve when traffic is left in the network.
Problems 1, 2 and 6 come from standard evaluation code.

% =============================================================================
\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}
The results show that phase numbering is the main obstacle to transfer across intersection
topologies in this setting, and that a phase-relational head that needs no configuration
removes it. The head belongs to an existing family of phase-invariant
designs~\cite{frap,attendlight}. Our contributions are the control experiment, the
automatically computed phase features, and the re-test of earlier results.

The study has these limitations.
\begin{itemize}
\item We did not compare with TransferLight~\cite{transferlight}.
\item We use one simulator and four real networks, and our training budgets are shorter
      than in published work.
\item Against max pressure, the head improves waiting time, not average delay. On the
      training networks it does not beat max pressure on both networks.
\item On \texttt{ingolstadt21}, the largest network, it is worse than both rule-based
      controllers.
\item The diversity result covers only topological diversity.
\item The FRAP results use a five-round checkpoint and do not describe MPLight as
      published.
\end{itemize}

% =============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
Numbering the outputs of a shared $Q$-network by phase prevents transfer across
intersection topologies in the setting studied. The evidence is: a control experiment that
rules out untrained rows; a gap that stays when training is up to four times longer; the
same effect with an independent phase-invariant method (FRAP); and the reversal of the two
strongest earlier interventions once the head is corrected. A phase-relational head, with
phase features computed from the simulator, needs no per-intersection configuration, keeps
the network's own signal program, transfers without retraining to unseen networks, and has
lower delay than the released code of a concurrent method. Negative results obtained with a
phase-numbered head in this setting should be re-checked before they are cited as evidence
about the mechanisms they tested.
