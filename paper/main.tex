\begin{abstract}
Reinforcement learning controllers for traffic signals are conventionally equipped
with a $Q$-head whose output row $k$ corresponds to phase index $k$. This paper
shows that such positional indexing is the binding constraint on cross-topology
generalization, and that its effect has been mistaken for the failure of unrelated
mechanisms. A control experiment isolates the defect: on a held-out network whose
phase count does not exceed the training maximum---so that every output row the
holdout can select is fully trained---an indexed head still fails, scoring
statistically indistinguishably from the case in which untrained rows are present.
A movement-relational readout, derived automatically from the simulator's own
topology and requiring no per-intersection configuration, closes most of the gap:
across five distinct configurations at six seeds each it outperforms the indexed
head by four orders of magnitude zero-shot on an unseen network, and reaches
$21.4$\,s average delay on the RESCO Cologne corridor against $22.13$\,s and
$23.99$\,s for published IPPO and IDQN, while training federated across three
cities at approximately one tenth of the reported budget. The separation is shown
to be independent of training budget, and a faithful port of MPLight's FRAP head
reproduces the mechanism from an independent implementation. Re-running the single
previously successful training-time intervention on the corrected readout reverses
its sign, which establishes empirically that roughly twenty prior null results in
this setting were floor effects rather than evidence about the mechanisms they
tested. Six distinct evaluation artifacts encountered during the study, each of
which produced a plausible but incorrect number, are documented.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Deep reinforcement learning applied to traffic signal control has repeatedly been
shown to improve over fixed-time and actuated control within the scenario on which
it was trained. Generalization across intersections of differing topology---a
single policy that controls a three-way junction and an eight-phase grid
intersection without retraining---remains substantially harder, and is the setting
studied here.

A shared policy over heterogeneous intersections requires a fixed-width output.
The standard construction pads every intersection's action space to a common
maximum and marks validity with a mask. Output row $k$ then denotes ``phase index
$k$'' at whichever intersection is currently being controlled. This is an
implementation convenience with a semantic consequence that has attracted little
scrutiny: phase orderings are authored independently per network, so row $k$ is
trained against conflicting targets. In the rosters used here, index~1 is a
protected left turn in two networks and a through movement in two others.

The contribution of this work is not a new architecture. Phase-invariant readouts
have existed since FRAP~\cite{frap}, and universal models handling arbitrary phase
counts since AttendLight~\cite{attendlight}. The contribution is a controlled
demonstration that the positional readout is the \emph{binding} constraint---not
one factor among several---together with the consequence that follows for how
negative results in this literature should be read.

\section{Related Work}

FRAP~\cite{frap} introduced phase competition, comparing phases pairwise so that
the model becomes invariant to flip and rotation, and reported transfer between
environments without additional training. MPLight~\cite{mplight} combines FRAP
with pressure as both state and reward and scales to thousands of signals.
AttendLight~\cite{attendlight} employs attention to accommodate any number of
phases, but qualifies its universality: the model applies ``as long as a similar
configuration is represented in the training set.''

Inductive graph formulations~\cite{igrl} and, more recently,
TransferLight~\cite{transferlight} target zero-shot deployment on unseen road
networks, the latter attributing its transfer to domain randomization over
randomly generated networks. Federated formulations for traffic control, including
hierarchical variants that cluster intersections before averaging~\cite{hfrl},
address privacy and scale rather than topological generalization.
RESCO~\cite{resco} provides the benchmark scenarios and the observation that
published methods frequently underperform simple baselines under realistic
conditions.

A distinction that proves material is drawn here between methods that are
phase-invariant and methods that are configuration-free. FRAP and MPLight require,
per scenario and per signal, a hand-authored table mapping local phase indices onto
a global movement vocabulary; the benchmark's own documentation states that
movements ``must be remapped to correspond to the same action in each agent.'' The
method described in Section~\ref{sec:system} derives that mapping from the
simulator, which is the property that distinguishes it.

\subsection{Why positional indexing cannot transfer}

Fig.~\ref{fig:semantics} makes the failure concrete. Under positional indexing the
same output row is asked to value unrelated movements in different networks; under
movement-relational indexing the readout is addressed by what a phase does, which
recurs across networks.

\section{Experimental Setup}

All experiments use SUMO with the RESCO scenario family. The training roster
comprises arterial4x4, cologne3 and ingolstadt7; grid4x4 is held out and never
trained on. Results are reported over six seeds unless stated otherwise;
three-seed results are labelled as screens and are not treated as confirmations.

Significance is reported as $|\Delta|/\mathrm{SE}$, computed unpaired with
population standard deviation. A value of $2$ is treated as the threshold.
Drop-one influence is reported so that a result carried by a single seed is
visible, and the sample-standard-deviation variant is given where a result is
sensitive to that choice.

Two protocol constraints are enforced throughout. Average delay and average trip
time are computed over \emph{arrived} vehicles only, so a controller that strands
traffic reports better numbers; throughput is therefore reported beside every delay
figure. Only scenario configurations matching the benchmark's own route files,
evaluation windows and $3$\,s yellow interval are used for external comparison.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The action representation is the binding constraint}\label{sec:control}

A prior measurement established that on unseen-topology rosters $37.5\%$ of the
holdout's action space is scored by output rows no training city ever exercises.
The obvious explanation for the indexed head's failure is therefore that those rows
remain at initialization. A control removes that explanation: the holdout is
replaced by a network whose phase count does not exceed the training maximum, so
that every row the holdout can select is fully trained.

\begin{table}[h]
\caption{Dead-row control, six seeds, holdout reward}
\label{tab:control}
\centering\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrl@{}}
\toprule
Condition & Indexed head & Interpretation \\
\midrule
Holdout with untrained rows ($8$ phases) & $-9296.84$ & defect present \\
Holdout without untrained rows ($4$ phases) & $-9028.88$ & defect removed \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Performance is statistically indistinguishable between the two conditions. The
untrained-row defect is therefore not the cause; the representation is.

\subsection{Zero-shot transfer to an unseen topology}

The movement-relational readout was confirmed at six seeds on five distinct
configurations: light demand, three-times demand, a four-phase holdout free of
untrained rows, benchmark-matched signal timing, and a fully benchmark-exact
roster. All six seeds favour it on every measure in each configuration, with
$|\Delta|/\mathrm{SE}$ between $23.6$ and $96.7$.

readout nonetheless outperforms FRAP on best-round across all six seeds while
requiring none of the per-signal configuration FRAP was given. The final-round
comparison against FRAP does not reach the threshold and is not claimed.

\subsection{Prior negative results are floor effects}\label{sec:floor}

Thirty distinct interventions were evaluated on this task before the readout was
identified as the constraint, spanning aggregation strategy, network architecture,
learning algorithm, loss shaping, training procedure and test-time adaptation.
Table~\ref{tab:inventory} is the complete inventory. Every entry was run under the
positionally indexed readout, on the same rosters and the same
$|\Delta|/\mathrm{SE}\geq 2$ criterion used elsewhere in this paper.

The distribution of outcomes is itself the evidence. Of thirty interventions,
twenty-one were null or negative, four were inconclusive, and five reached the
threshold---of which three were test-time or evaluation-time procedures that do not
alter the learned policy, and one, discussed below, reverses when the readout is
corrected. No training-time change to architecture, algorithm, aggregation or loss
produced a durable improvement. A search of that breadth returning that result is
the signature of a floor, not of thirty independently unhelpful mechanisms.

Of the interventions that did clear the threshold, the strongest was a progressive
curriculum with per-city fine-tuning. Re-running it on the corrected readout
reverses its sign.

\subsection{A structural limitation of pressure-only state}

FRAP performs poorly in-distribution despite transferring well zero-shot. Its
entire state is per-movement pressure---inbound queue minus downstream
queue---which degenerates to plain queue length wherever a movement has no
downstream lanes inside the network. Coverage measured from the benchmark's own
wiring tracks its results across all four networks: $75\%$ on grid4x4 and
arterial4x4, where FRAP is strong; $39\%$ on ingolstadt7 and $33\%$ on cologne3,
where it is weak and weakest respectively. Corridor networks, in which most
movements discharge out of the modelled area, deprive a pressure-only state of its
signal. This is a property of the state design rather than of the port, since
pressure is computed as the reference implementation computes it. The relationship
is offered as a supported hypothesis rather than a demonstrated mechanism: it rests
on four networks, is confounded with training budget, and the isolating experiment
has not been run.

\section{Methodological Findings}

Six evaluation artifacts were encountered, each of which produced a plausible but
incorrect number. They are reported because several are properties of widely used
tooling rather than of this implementation.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textit{Silent holdout substitution.} When a reduced roster's shared output
      width is narrower than the held-out network's action space, the evaluator
      falls back to a training city without failing, yielding an in-distribution
      number labelled as cross-topology.
\item \textit{Holdout topology leakage.} A roster contained a training city whose
      network file was identical to the holdout's; name-based holdout checks do not
      detect this.
\item \textit{Untrained output rows.} $37.5\%$ of a holdout's action space scored by
      rows no training city exercised.
\item \textit{Scenario configuration drift.} Route files, evaluation windows and
      yellow interval all differed from the benchmark's own; a $2$\,s rather than
      $3$\,s yellow grants every controller $50\%$ more usable green per phase
      change at a $5$\,s action interval.
\item \textit{A missing signal phase.} A vendored network omitted one green phase
      present in the benchmark's copy, reducing one intersection's action space.
\item \textit{Survivorship in delay metrics.} Delay and trip time are computed over
      arrived vehicles, so stranding traffic improves them; this inverted one
      comparison before throughput was reported alongside.
\end{enumerate}

A further observation concerns seed counts: five interventions in this study
produced clean, unanimous three-seed results that reversed or evaporated at six
seeds. Three seeds are treated here as a screen only.

% =============================================================================
\section{Limitations}

The proposed readout is not a novel architecture; phase-invariant readouts predate
it, and the contribution rests on the control of Section~\ref{sec:control} and its
consequence. No comparison against a current zero-shot method has been performed,
so competitiveness with the recent state of the art is unestablished. Evaluation
uses a single simulator and small rosters, and training budgets are short relative
to published practice. An attempt to establish whether training-topology diversity
improves robustness was inconclusive because the holdout was saturated at the
budget used, and is reported as a failed measurement rather than a null result.
The published comparison on ingolstadt7 is additionally qualified by artifact~5.

% =============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

Positional indexing of a shared $Q$-head is the binding constraint on
cross-topology generalization in this setting, and not one contributing factor
among several. The claim is supported by a control that eliminates the
untrained-row explanation, by the persistence of the separation under a four-fold
budget increase, by independent reproduction through a published phase-invariant
readout, and by the reversal of the one previously successful intervention once the
readout is corrected. The practical consequence is that negative results obtained
in this setting through a positionally indexed readout do not constitute evidence
about the mechanisms they were designed to test.
