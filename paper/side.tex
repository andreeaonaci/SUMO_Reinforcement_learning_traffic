\section{The problem we want to solve}
Traffic lights controlled by reinforcement learning can reduce waiting at the
intersection they were trained on. What cities would actually need is a controller
that can be trained once, on a few instrumented intersections, and then installed at
new intersections without training it again. This is called \emph{zero-shot transfer}.

The difficulty is that intersections are not alike. Some have two signal phases,
some have eight. A phase is one green configuration, for example ``north and south
go straight'' or ``east turns left''. We want a single learned policy, trained with
federated learning across several cities, that can control any of them and still
work on a city it has never seen.

In this project that policy failed on unseen networks for a long time, and many
reasonable fixes did not help. The paper explains why, shows how to fix it, and
tests the fix carefully.

\section{The phase problem}

\subsection{Explained simply}
Imagine several street crossings, each with a panel of buttons that change the
lights. On the first crossing, button 1 lets cars turn left. On the second crossing,
button 1 lets cars go straight. A robot learns from both crossings and tries to
remember ``button 1 is a good idea when many cars are waiting''. It becomes
confused, because button 1 does a different thing in each place. When it arrives at
a new crossing, it has no idea what button 1 does there.

A better robot does not remember button numbers at all. Instead, each button carries
a small label that says what it does: ``lets 12 waiting cars turn left'' or ``lets 3
cars go straight''. The robot learns to read labels. Now it can use a crossing it has
never seen, because the labels mean the same thing everywhere.

\subsection{Stated formally}
A shared policy must output one value per action. Since intersection $i$ has $A_i$
phases and $A_i$ varies, the usual construction pads the output to
$A_{\max}=\max_i A_i$ and masks the actions that do not exist:
\[
Q_\theta(o_i)=W\,h_\theta(o_i)+b,\qquad
a_i=\arg\max_{k:\,m_i[k]=1} Q_\theta(o_i)[k].
\]
Row $W_k$ is the only parameter that values ``action $k$'' everywhere. Let
$\sigma_i(k)$ be the set of movements phase $k$ serves at intersection $i$. The phase
order in a SUMO signal program is chosen by whoever built the network, so
$\sigma_i(k)$ differs across networks. In the four RESCO benchmark networks we use,
phase 1 is a protected left turn in two of them and a through movement in the other
two. Row $W_1$ is therefore trained on contradictory targets and carries no meaning
that transfers.

There is a second, simpler explanation to rule out. When the unseen network has more
phases than any training city, some rows are never trained. On our standard holdout
this affects 37.5\% of its actions. The two explanations need different fixes, so we
separated them with a control experiment (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\section{The idea: describe phases, do not number them}
We replace the output layer with a scorer that rates each candidate phase from a
description of what it does (Figure~\ref{fig:model}):
\[
Q_\theta(o_i)[a]=f_\psi\big([\,h_\theta(o_i)\,\|\,\phi_i(a)\,]\big),
\qquad \phi_i(a)\in\mathbb{R}^{10}.
\]
The descriptor $\phi_i(a)$ holds the phase's pressure (incoming minus outgoing
queue), its queue, waiting time, occupancy and lane count, whether it is currently
shown and for how long, and the share of its green lanes that go straight, left and
right. All of it is computed from SUMO's controlled links table, so a new
intersection needs no manual setup.

This has four practical consequences. The number of parameters no longer depends on
the number of phases, so there are no untrained rows. There is no index whose
meaning could conflict between cities. The policy works for any phase count, including
counts not seen in training. And because $\phi_i(a)$ includes per phase pressure, the
classic max pressure rule is inside what the model can express, which was not true
of the indexed head.

The closest earlier methods, FRAP and MPLight, also avoid phase numbers, but they
need a hand written table that maps each intersection's phases to movements before
they can control it. Our descriptor is built automatically. This is the property we
claim, rather than a new architecture.

\section{What we found}\label{sec:results}
Results are over six random seeds unless stated. We call a difference significant
when $|\Delta|/\mathrm{SE}\ge 2$. Delay is computed over vehicles that arrive, so we
always give the completion rate next to it.

\paragraph{The representation is the cause.}
We built a holdout whose phase count does not exceed the training maximum, so every
row of the indexed head is fully trained. The indexed head still gridlocks: reward
$-9028.88$, compared with $-9296.84$ when untrained rows are present. The difference
is within seed noise. Untrained rows were not the problem; the numbering was.

\paragraph{Zero-shot transfer.}
On an unseen network, the phase-relational readout outperforms the indexed head by
four orders of magnitude, on five configurations, with every seed agreeing and
$|\Delta|/\mathrm{SE}$ between 23.6 and 96.7. Table~\ref{tab:zs} shows the two
configurations that match the RESCO benchmark's own settings. The indexed head
completes only 16 to 20\% of trips on the unseen network.

\paragraph{Compared with max pressure.}
On the same unseen network, the learned policy has lower waiting time than max
pressure on all six seeds (18.8\,s against 22.8\,s) while completing the same share
of trips (98.8\% against 99.2\%). On average delay the two are tied (41.4\,s against
40.2\,s). This fits a policy trained on a waiting time reward.

\paragraph{More training does not rescue the indexed head.}
With two and a half to four times the budget, the indexed head is still four orders
of magnitude behind. FRAP, which we ported from RESCO's code and gave the hand
written table for every network, also transfers. Our readout reaches FRAP's level
without the table: it is better on the best round for all six seeds, while the final
round difference is not significant.

\paragraph{On RESCO's own Cologne scenario.}
Trained federated across three cities, the policy reaches 21.4\,s average delay and
58.9\,s trip time, with 91.6\% of trips completed. For reference, max pressure gives
22.4\,s at 98.6\%, and the published RESCO results are 22.13\,s for IPPO and 23.99\,s
for IDQN. RESCO does not report completion rates, so we describe this as comparable
performance rather than a clear win.

\paragraph{Compared with concurrent work.}
Braun (arXiv 2607.21831, July 2026) independently proposed a related idea, scoring
movements instead of phase numbers. We ran his released code on the same networks and
measured everything with one pipeline. On the unseen network his method gives
293\,s average delay (94.4\% completed) against our 41.4\,s (98.8\%), and it is also
behind in Cologne and Ingolstadt. This is a three seed screen, and his method was
designed for a different metric (throughput) and different networks, so we present
it as a comparison on our benchmark only.

\paragraph{Earlier negative results, explained.}
Before finding the cause, we tested 41 other changes: aggregation rules,
architectures, algorithms, reward shaping, curricula and fine tuning. Most gave
nothing. We then repeated the two strongest ones with the new readout. Both stopped
helping: a training curriculum is now beaten by plain averaging on all six seeds, and
fine tuning on the new network, which earlier gave the largest gain of all, now makes
the policy slightly worse. This suggests those earlier experiments were limited by
the numbering problem, rather than showing that the ideas themselves do not work.

\paragraph{Evaluation pitfalls.}
We document six measurement problems that each produced a believable but wrong
number, such as an evaluator that silently tested on a training city, and a
simulator setting (2\,s instead of 3\,s yellow) that gave every controller more green
time than the benchmark allows. Three of them come from common tooling.

\section{Limitations}
The readout is not a new architecture in itself; related phase based designs exist
(FRAP, AttendLight), and zero-shot transfer and federated signal control have been
studied before. On the networks it was trained on, the policy does not beat max
pressure everywhere: it ties on Cologne and is behind on Ingolstadt (35.2\,s against
26.6\,s, although it completes more trips). We have not run a budget matched
comparison with TransferLight, and the Braun comparison is being extended from three
to six seeds.

\section{Where to submit}
The draft is written as a method and evaluation paper, not a leaderboard paper: a
control experiment that identifies the cause, a configuration free readout that
removes it, a new reading of 41 earlier experiments, and six evaluation pitfalls. It
currently needs about ten pages in IEEE format. A transportation venue would value
the configuration free controller and the RESCO results. A machine learning venue
with a benchmarking or methodology focus would value the control experiment and the
evaluation pitfalls. A conference version would move the full list of experiments to
supplementary material, while a journal could keep everything. Advice on which
