\title{\textbf{Phase-Relational $Q$-Learning for Cross-Topology Traffic Signal Control}\\[4pt]
\large Briefing: what was done, where it wins, and where to submit}
\author{Author Name}
\date{September 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
A single reinforcement-learning policy that controls intersections of \emph{different}
geometry (2 to 8 signal phases) has to output a fixed-width action vector. The standard way
to do that binds output row $k$ to ``phase number $k$'', but phase numbers are arbitrary and
mean different things in different networks. We show, with a control experiment, that this
binding is the main reason such policies fail on an unseen network. Replacing it with a
\emph{phase-relational} readout---scoring each phase from a description of what it does,
derived automatically from the simulator---takes zero-shot performance on an unseen network
from gridlock to better than max pressure on waiting time. The result is confirmed at six
seeds on five configurations, at the RESCO benchmark's own settings, and against the
concurrent method closest to ours. The draft paper is complete (IEEE format, 10 pages,
\texttt{paper/main.tex}). This note summarises it and asks where to submit.
\end{abstract}

% =============================================================================
\section{The problem in one paragraph}
Federated training produces one shared DQN for every intersection in several cities, with
the goal of deploying it zero-shot on a network it has never seen. Because intersections
have different numbers of phases, the $Q$-head is padded to the largest phase count, and
actions that do not exist are masked out. Row $k$ of the output layer therefore learns ``the
value of phase $k$''---but the phase order in a SUMO signal program is chosen by whoever
built the network. In the four RESCO networks we use, phase~1 is a \emph{protected left
turn} in two of them and a \emph{through movement} in the other two. One weight vector is
trained on contradictory targets (Fig.~\ref{fig:collision}), so it has no transferable
meaning.

\section{The idea: phase-relational readout}
Instead of one output row per phase \emph{index}, the network scores each candidate phase
from a description of what that phase \emph{does}:
\[
Q_\theta(o_i)[a] \;=\; f_\psi\big(\,[\,h_\theta(o_i)\;\|\;\phi_i(a)\,]\,\big),
\qquad \phi_i(a)\in\mathbb{R}^{10},
\]
where $h_\theta$ is the shared state embedding and $\phi_i(a)$ is a 10-number descriptor of
phase $a$ at intersection $i$. The descriptor holds the phase's pressure (incoming minus
outgoing queue), queue, waiting time, occupancy and lane count; whether the phase is
currently shown and for how long; and the fraction of its green lanes that go straight, left
and right. It is computed \textbf{automatically} from SUMO's controlled-links table, so
nothing has to be written by hand for a new intersection.

\noindent Why it matters:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No per-action parameters.} Widening the action space adds nothing, so there are
      no untrained rows and no indices whose meaning can conflict.
\item \textbf{Works for any phase count}, including counts never seen in training.
\item \textbf{Strictly more expressive.} Because $\phi_i(a)$ contains per-phase pressure, the
      max-pressure rule lies \emph{inside} the model class. It did not under the indexed head.
\item \textbf{Configuration-free.} FRAP and MPLight cannot control an intersection until
      someone writes its phase-to-movement table (RESCO ships three such hand-written
      dictionaries for Cologne alone). Ours derives the equivalent at run time.
\end{itemize}

\section{Where we win}
All results are six seeds unless stated. Significance is $|\Delta|/\mathrm{SE}$ (unpaired);
$\ge 2$ is the threshold used throughout. Delay and trip time are computed over
\emph{arrived} vehicles only, so they are always given with a completion rate.

\subsection*{W1. The control experiment: the representation, not undertrained rows \sect{98}}
On the standard holdout, 37.5\% of the unseen network's actions are scored by rows no training
city ever used---an obvious alternative explanation. We built a holdout whose phase count does
not exceed the training maximum, so every row is fully trained. The indexed head \emph{still}
gridlocks: $-9028.88$ vs.\ $-9296.84$ with untrained rows, a difference within seed noise.
This is the paper's central result: it rules out the cheap fixes (wider head, more
training cities) and pins the failure on the representation.

\subsection*{W2. Zero-shot on an unseen network: four orders of magnitude \sect{96--100}}
Confirmed at six seeds on five configurations (light demand, $3\times$ demand, the
control holdout, benchmark timing, fully benchmark-exact roster). All six seeds favour
phase-relational on every measure; $|\Delta|/\mathrm{SE}$ ranges from 23.6 to 96.7.

\subsection*{W3. Better than max pressure zero-shot---on waiting time \sect{109, 109b}}
At the benchmark's own 3\,s signal timing, phase-relational beats max pressure on
\textbf{every seed, at best and final round}, on both benchmark-timed rosters, at matched
throughput (98.9\% vs.\ 99.2\% completion). Waiting time, measured from trip records: 18.8\,s
vs.\ 22.8\,s, lower on 6/6 seeds. \emph{On average delay the two are tied} (41.4 vs.\ 40.2\,s),
consistent with a policy trained on a waiting-time reward. On two further holdouts
(internal comparisons at 2\,s yellow, not comparable with published numbers): at $3\times$
demand the reward is $-0.32$ vs.\ max pressure's $-0.640$ and fixed time's $-170.51$, and on
the control holdout $-1.38$ vs.\ $-2.225$.

\subsection*{W4. Survives a $2.5$--$4\times$ larger training budget, and matches FRAP without its configuration \sect{103}}
At 20 rounds the indexed head is still four orders of magnitude behind ($-4992.77$ /
$-6387.32$ vs.\ $-0.082$ / $-0.175$; 5.57 / 13.32), and the final-round gap is \emph{wider}
than the best-round gap---the opposite of what undertraining predicts. FRAP, ported from
RESCO's source and \emph{given the hand-written table for every network, including the
holdout}, also transfers ($-0.253$), which independently confirms the mechanism.
Phase-relational beats it on best round on 6/6 seeds (4.06), with no table at all; the
final-round comparison is not significant (1.71), so this is parity with a configuration-free
method, not superiority. It is also
far more stable: FRAP degrades $3.49\times$ from its best, against $2.19\times$ for ours.

\subsection*{W7. A body of negative results, reinterpreted \sect{102, 105, inventory}}
Before the representation was identified, \textbf{41 interventions} were tested with the
indexed head: aggregation rules, architectures, PPO / QR-DQN / CQL / MAML, loss shaping,
curricula and fine-tuning. Thirty-two were null or negative, 4 were inconclusive, and 5
cleared the bar; the training-time ones among those were modest. The two strongest positives
were re-run with the corrected readout, and \textbf{both reverse} (six seeds each):
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Progressive curriculum} (the ordered-client idea of Vahidian et al., ICCV 2023):
      plain averaging beats both curriculum arms on 6/6 seeds.
\item \emph{Fine-tuning on the target topology}, the largest positive in the whole corpus
      ($|\Delta|/\mathrm{SE}=72.78$ with the indexed head): now it \emph{hurts} ($-0.123 \to
      -0.250$, 4.44). The one seed where it helps is the one with the worst starting policy;
      the correlation is $r=-0.91$.
\end{itemize}
This turns ``many things don't work'' into a measured finding: those experiments were
measuring the representational floor, not the ideas they were testing.

\subsection*{W8. Six evaluation artifacts, each of which produced a plausible wrong number \sect{25, 95, 99, 100b, 101b}}
These are silent holdout substitution by the evaluator; a training city sharing the holdout's
network file; untrained output rows; scenario drift from RESCO (route files, windows, and a
2\,s vs.\ 3\,s yellow that gives every controller 50\% more green); a missing green phase in a
vendored network; and survivorship in delay metrics. Three of the six are properties of
standard tooling, so they matter beyond this project.

\section{Where we do \emph{not} win (so the positioning is right)}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Not a novel architecture.} Phase-invariant readouts exist (FRAP 2019, AttendLight
      2020), as do zero-shot cross-topology transfer (TransferLight 2024) and federated signal
      control (HFRL 2025). Braun 2026 is concurrent. The novelty is the \emph{control
      experiment}, the \emph{configuration-free} property, and the \emph{reinterpretation} of
      the negative results.
\item \textbf{In-distribution we do not beat max pressure everywhere.} We tie it on Cologne delay
      and lose on Ingolstadt (35.2 vs.\ 26.6\,s delay, although ours completes the most traffic,
      95.4\%). On zero-shot delay we tie it.
\item \textbf{No budget-matched comparison against TransferLight}, and no claim of state of the art.
\item Some side results are three-seed screens: the Braun comparison (six seeds finishing) and
      the checkpoint ensemble.
\end{itemize}

\section{Where could this go? Questions for you}
The draft's framing, decided after a prior-art review \sect{101}: \emph{a mechanism and
evaluation-methodology paper}. It pairs a control experiment identifying the binding
constraint with a configuration-free readout that removes it, a reinterpretation of 41 prior
interventions, and six evaluation artifacts. It is deliberately not framed as a
leaderboard paper.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Conference vs.\ journal.} The full programme currently needs about 10 IEEE pages. A
      conference version would move the intervention corpus and the artifacts to supplementary
      material; a journal (e.g.\ an intelligent-transportation journal) could carry all of it.
\item \textbf{Transportation venue vs.\ ML venue.} The configuration-free readout and the RESCO
      results speak to intelligent-transportation readers. The control experiment, the floor-effect
      reinterpretation and the evaluation artifacts speak to an ML methodology or benchmarking
      track (RESCO itself appeared in a datasets-and-benchmarks track).
\item \textbf{What would most strengthen it for a stronger venue?} Candidates: a budget-matched
      TransferLight run; the Braun comparison at six seeds (running now); real-demand rather than
      synthetic-demand fine-tuning; a fifth, fully unseen real-world network.
\end{itemize}

\section{The journey}
The work is recorded as a dated lab notebook (\texttt{fidings/divergence\_investigation.md},
§1--§110), and every number in this note traces to a section of it.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Infrastructure (Phase~0).} A federated DQN with persistent per-city workers,
      Double DQN, target networks, masked heads, and a true-holdout evaluator. Run-to-run
      non-determinism was found and characterised early \sect{3}.
\item \textbf{The symptom: ``confident lock-in''} \sect{32--57}. Trained policies collapsed into
      degenerate, highly confident behaviour, with byte-identical rewards across 30 different
      simulator seeds. Confidence (the $Q$-gap) correlated $-0.884$ with reward. Removing
      federation did not remove it (lock-in rate 7\% federated vs.\ 6\% independent).
\item \textbf{Confound hunting} \sect{43, 58--61}. The holdout had silently been a training city
      (artifact~1). Once that was fixed, a larger training budget closed the gap
      \emph{in-distribution} but not across topologies, which separated undertraining from a real
      generalisation gap.
\item \textbf{41 interventions} \sect{62--95}. Aggregation strategies, clustering, architectures,
      algorithms, loss shaping, curricula and fine-tuning were all tested. Fine-tuning looked
      strongest, until a randomly initialised control matched it \sect{70}.
\item \textbf{The representation hypothesis} \sect{95--98}. Untrained rows were found and then ruled
      out by the control experiment; the phase-relational readout was introduced and confirmed.
\item \textbf{Measurement corrections} \sect{99--101b}. Three mismatches with RESCO's
      configuration were found, including the 2\,s vs.\ 3\,s yellow, and one earlier external
      claim was retracted. A prior-art review then fixed the positioning.
\item \textbf{Confirmations and baselines} \sect{102--110}. The FRAP port, the budget test, both
      reversals, the diversity null, the rule-based references at benchmark timing, and Braun's
      method.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{B.3 Training-topology diversity \sect{104, 108, 108b}}
Four synthetic irregular grids were added to the three real training cities, and the model
was evaluated on the triple-demand holdout (which has $\sim$10$\times$ the dynamic range of the
original holdout). Over six seeds, no measure favours diversity: best round 0.58, final 0.20,
mean 1.60 (in $|\Delta|/\mathrm{SE}$), and at most 2 of 6 seeds favour it. The detectable
effect was 0.049 against a range of 1.082, so the test had the power to see an effect.
\emph{More topological diversity does not help.}

\subsection*{B.4 Ensembles \sect{93, 106} (screen)}
A majority vote over six independently trained checkpoints ties the best member ($-0.12$) and
beats the member mean ($-0.172$, 2.20 SE); its value is \emph{selection} without a holdout.
Averaging the weights of independent seeds collapses the policy ($-3.87$): the runs sit in
different loss basins. This does not affect FedAvg, which averages from a common point every
round.

\section{How the results were kept honest}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Six seeds} for every confirmation; three-seed results are labelled screens. Five
      interventions produced clean three-seed wins that reversed or vanished at six.
\item \textbf{Drop-one influence} is reported, so a result carried by a single seed is visible.
\item \textbf{A provenance table} in the paper records, for every result, the roster, the yellow
      interval, and whether it may be compared with published numbers.
\item \textbf{Completion is reported beside every delay}, and every ``beats X'' names its metric.
\item \textbf{A pre-submission audit} re-derived all 80 result numbers from raw data, verified
      every citation against its source (a citation written from memory had named an author who
      does not exist), and found the concurrent Braun paper.
\end{itemize}
